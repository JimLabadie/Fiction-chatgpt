# St. Claire — Rules & Mechanics

This document contains generative rules, checks, and mechanics — the toolkit used to create new content consistently, not settled narrative fact. If a fictional St. Claire resident wouldn't know or care about something, it belongs here, not in St_Claire_00_Concept.md (Lore). Concept holds why/how and settled history; this document holds the checkable rules derived from those values. Data documents (Population, Households, Organizations, Places) hold pure information — no rules, no reasoning, just the facts themselves.

---

## Governing Process Rules (non-negotiable)

- **St_Claire_00_Concept.md is the source of truth for lore. This document is the source of truth for mechanics.** Everything else — the Master Lore Compendium, character documents, any story bible — implements what's decided in these two documents, and does not override them. If a downstream document contradicts either one, the downstream document is wrong and gets corrected to match.
- **Any checkable ratio, threshold, default, or behavioral rule that appears in Concept.md's narrative prose gets extracted into this document, not left there.** This is the same promotion principle already established for the Master Lore Compendium below, applied to the Concept/Mechanics boundary itself: Concept.md keeps the *why* — reasoning, history, tone — but the checkable fact itself belongs here, verbatim or close to it, so this document alone is sufficient to answer any checkable question without needing to search Concept.md's prose. This is a standing rule, not a one-time cleanup: any future addition to Concept.md gets checked against this boundary before being considered finished, the same way a new population batch gets checked against the demographic ratios before being considered finished.
- **Exceptions belong to individual stories, not to the world.** A specific story can bend a rule established here — break a pattern, put a character somewhere the world wouldn't normally allow, override an established default — and that stays scoped to that story alone. It does not get absorbed back into these documents as a new general truth.
- **The Master Lore Compendium is a quarry, not a maintained document.** It's raw material to draw from — checked against duplicates like everything else — but once something is promoted out of it into a structured document (Population, Households, Organizations, Places), and only after verification that every relevant detail scattered across the compendium has actually been captured, it is deleted from the compendium entirely, not left in place with a note. Verification is not optional. The structured document becomes the only place that fact is ever updated again.
- No invented community-wide naming gimmicks or "traditions" fabricated to retroactively explain AI mistakes (e.g., the "Vance" surname device — cut, not canon).
- No AI-hallucinated lore treated as settled fact. If it wasn't decided in conversation or drawn from the author's original drafts, it doesn't go in.
- Real, distinct people — no reused names for different characters, no unearned repetition standing in for texture.
- **Population happens in two separate steps, never one.** Step 1: define what professions/institutions/roles exist (structural — a list, no names). Step 2: name individual people, in small batches, one category at a time, checked against everyone already named before adding more. Never generate a large named cast in a single pass.
- The neighborhood is idealistic and progressive, but not frictionless. Institutions have real limits (waitlists, funding gaps, capacity). The professional class includes ordinary necessary roles, not just caretaking ones. Real-estate and development pressure exists and creates real tension. **Shorthand for this arc: the church, then the lesbian "takeover," then reality hitting with a 2x4.** Every idealistic element in Concept should be able to survive that third beat.
- **Never write meta-commentary about the real-world editorial/creation process into any lore or data document.** In-world content should read as settled fact the inhabitants would recognize as their own history — never "this was corrected," "previously missing," "before this session," or similar. That kind of language belongs in the Tracker only.
- **Only one tracker exists — St_Claire_99_MASTER_TRACKER.md.** No topic-specific sub-trackers, regardless of how large or complex a thread becomes.

## Demographic Ratios — checked against every new population batch

**Presentation:** ~48% femme / ~30% androgynous / ~21% butch / ~1% other.

**Ethnicity:** matched to real US Census baseline — ~53% white, 17% Latina/Latine/Latino, 14% Black, 6% East Asian, 4% South Asian, ~2% Middle Eastern, <1% each Southeast Asian and Native/Indigenous.

**Trans/cis:** ~11% trans, ~89% cis. Trans women carry real weight within that 11% — the largest single trans subgroup, not an equal three-way split with trans men and non-binary residents.

**Relationship status:** ~54% partnered (couples), ~28% single, ~12% triad, ~8% quad. Established specifically to stop "single" from becoming an accidental majority when bulk business-staffing math defaults new hires to single for efficiency — a real failure mode this ratio exists to prevent.

**Pet ownership:** real LGBTQ-specific rates run 70-78.5%, higher than the general population's ~60-63% (Harris Interactive, GLCensus). Cat preference is documented and specific to this population, not just stereotype — queer women show a real inversion of the general population's dog-lean (Autostraddle's lesbian-specific survey; 63% LGBT cat ownership vs. 52% heterosexual, only 63% LGBT dog ownership vs. 71% heterosexual). House-type/affluent residences get only a modest dog-probability bump, not a dominant one. Exotic/"other" pets (fish, birds, reptiles, small mammals) are ~14.2% of pet-owning households nationally, skewing toward young urban apartment/condo dwellers specifically.

**Polycules and pets — a reasoned extension, not a directly-studied correlation.** No study addresses triad/quad households specifically, but larger households own measurably more pets on average in general population data (rural households at 2.8 pets vs. urban's 1.4), and more adults means genuinely distributed caretaking capacity and pooled income — the two real factors that most suppress pet ownership. Triads and quads should show pet ownership at or above couples, and are the most plausible households for a shared "family pet" with genuinely distributed care.

**Household vehicles/transportation — cross-referenced against real household composition, not just economic tier.** Real data: 44% of families with children choose SUVs vs. only 5% minivans (minivans win only for genuinely larger families, 3+ kids); multi-vehicle ownership scales with both income (20.4% to 81.5% by bracket) and number of adults, making polycules genuinely more likely to be multi-vehicle households; motorcycles carry real, documented cultural weight tied to butch identity specifically (Dykes on Bikes, founded 1976 SF Pride — 22 chapters nationally, women's motorcycle ownership at 19% and climbing). St. Claire's own car-dependent baseline applies district-wide, with the walkable womb-core zones showing a real, higher car-free rate. Recreational vehicles (RVs, boats) appear as a modest, real minority presence, not absent.

**Trans dating realities — real, sourced grounding, worth codifying given trans representation's real prevalence in stories set here (~11% of the population per the ratio above).** Transgender women specifically report more difficulty finding romantic partners than other transgender/nonbinary people (Fuller & Riggs, 2021; Riggs et al., 2015). Roughly 70% of transgender/nonbinary people report difficulty finding a partner because of their gender identity or expression (Full Article, dating goals/relationship structures study, 2024). Across a mixed-orientation sample, 87.5% indicated they would not consider dating a trans person at all; among heterosexual respondents specifically, only 3% would (Blair & Hoskin, 2019). Of trans women who do date, 63% report experiencing fetishization rather than genuine interest, the highest rate among any gender group studied. **The story implication, not just the statistic:** a partner who reacts to a trans woman's disclosure with genuine, unhedged acceptance — no hesitation, no fetishizing curiosity, treating her as simply, fully real — represents a real, statistically rare outcome, not a narrative nicety. A trans character recognizing this and moving with more confidence or urgency than caution alone would predict is psychologically accurate given how rare a clean good outcome actually is, not narrative convenience overriding realism.

**How this actually works in practice:** any time a new batch of characters gets built — staffing a new business, populating a new residential building, whatever the reason — the batch should be checked against all ratios above before being finalized. A batch of 5-10 people will naturally have some variance and that's fine; the check matters at the aggregate population level, where drift compounds silently if nobody's watching for it.

## Date Precision — every date is a full date, but deliberation varies by type

**Every date in every document should be a full date (month, day, year) — no exceptions, no year-only or month-year-only entries.** The reason isn't accuracy (many of these dates are arbitrary either way) — it's that any date might matter to a story that hasn't been written yet, and having it exist as a complete date costs nothing now while saving real trouble later.

**What differs by date type is how much deliberation goes into choosing the specific date, not whether it gets full precision:**

- **Individual dates** (birthdates, individual deaths not tied to a communal event) belong to one person, need no coordination with anyone else, and can be assigned efficiently — any reasonable date works, no special thought required about which specific day.
- **Historical/communal dates** (Genesis Day, the 1974 squat, the 1976 womb plan, the 1988 zoning loss, any date the community collectively marks or references) need genuine deliberation — the specific date should carry real meaning. Genesis Day is March 8th *because* that's International Women's Day, not because any day would do.
- **Relational dates** (anniversaries, weddings, household formation) belong to the relationship, not to each person separately — every person who was part of that specific event should show the identical date, not independently-generated dates that happen to be close.
- **Polycule relational dates are often plural, not singular.** Real polyamorous households usually form in stages rather than everyone arriving together on day one. A couple becoming a throuple is one date; a fourth person joining later is a second, later date; two established couples merging into a quad is a distinct merger date. Each transition date is shared only among the specific people who were part of that transition — earlier dates (an original couple's own anniversary) stay intact underneath later ones rather than being overwritten.

**Known current violation, not yet corrected:** roughly 263 of 407 birthdates in Population.md are currently year-only. This is a real, large-scale backlog — flagged in the Tracker, not fixed in this pass.

## Womb Sizing Principles

Real urban-planning walkability standards, not arbitrary geometry:

- **Circulatory Rings** (the daily-use walkable core around any womb's Inner Chamber): sized to the real 5-minute walk "pedestrian shed" standard — a quarter-mile radius, roughly 120-160 acres, the classic New Urbanism neighborhood unit. A womb with more population than a single walkshed can hold gets multiple smaller rings, not one oversized one.
- **Umbilical Roads** (connecting one womb's Inner Chamber to another's): sized to the real 15-minute bike-shed distance, roughly 3-4x the walking radius — consistent with Umbilical Roads being the one mobility layer built for anything faster than walking/slow cycling.
- **Perimeter Loop:** long enough to encompass the whole district as a single outer ring — a longer-distance route, not a daily-use path.
- **Relative zone size** should track the district's real established population distribution (Residential Infill ~28%, Premium Riverfront ~23%, Working Riverfront ~20%, The Hills ~15%, Transition Zone ~10%, Downtown ~5%) rather than being redrawn arbitrarily.

## Information Flow Between Characters

Characters not present in a scene do not know what happened in that scene unless a character who was present actually tells them, on the page or explicitly stated as having happened off-page. No character references private knowledge from a scene they weren't in without a stated transmission path. This includes gossip — gossip requires an actual originating conversation, not ambient knowledge.

## Direct Naming, Not Hedged Avoidance

When a character in-story has real reason to think someone may be transgender, the word "transgender" (or "trans woman" / "trans man" as specific) gets used directly, not circled with euphemism ("a real possibility," "something underneath," "a pattern I've seen"). The established real pattern — many trans people don't yet know they're trans, and only find out through someone naming the possibility directly — is a real, worth-representing phenomenon, not something to imply without stating.

**Failure this rule exists to prevent:** a story used the felt-pull mechanic (Concept.md, "A real, felt pull toward St. Claire, for trans women specifically") on a character not yet consciously aware — but rendered the entire thing as ambient interior sensation (unnamed warmth, an unexplained reluctance to leave, a feeling he couldn't name) with no resident ever actually saying anything to him. The rule above was never technically violated, because no character was ever written as having "real reason to think" anything — the scene simply avoided ever reaching that trigger condition. The result read as withholding rather than care: the character was left alone with something unnamed instead of being met by the "warm, instinctive recognition from residents who've been there themselves" the mechanic is specifically built on.

**The actual rule, going forward:** if a story invokes this mechanic on an unaware character, it must include at least one resident who has actual standing to recognize it (per Concept.md — someone who has been through it herself, not a stranger guessing) actually saying something to him on the page, using the direct word, before the scene resolves. Ambient mood, unexplained pull, or a feeling he "can't name" is allowed as texture leading up to that moment, but is never a substitute for it and never the scene's endpoint on its own. The naming does not have to resolve anything — he can leave without deciding — but it has to actually happen, on the page, spoken by someone, not left as something only the reader is trusted to infer.

## Recognition and Disclosure Pacing

**Failure this rule exists to prevent:** a resident's read on a near-stranger got acted on within a single scene, off a single striking line of dialogue — the same shape of error whether it's a resident naming something about someone else, or (as a later test surfaced) whether it's a character's own significant disclosure getting rushed to arrive too easily. One good sentence is not the same as earned trust or accumulated evidence, regardless of how well-written the sentence is.

**The actual rule, three real conditions:**
1. **The character needs solitary processing time before any external read carries real weight on the page.** If a character has had a significant internal experience (a felt pull, a realization, anything load-bearing), that needs its own space — alone, interior — before another character's read on it is treated as meaningful. Skipping straight from the experience into someone else noticing it externally undercuts both.
2. **A read or suspicion needs multiple accumulated concrete signals, not one poetic line, before anyone acts on it.** A single well-written sentence of dialogue is one data point. Real suspicion about something significant builds from a pattern noticed over time, not a single striking moment an hour into knowing someone.
3. **Two-step structure, not one scene carrying everything.** A lighter, deniable gesture or tease can happen early (consistent with the existing "egg" slang rule, which already allows this). The actual direct commitment — naming something outright, or extending deep trust — gets deferred to a later scene, once real accumulated trust and pattern actually exist. A story is allowed to end a scene on the lighter, held-back version and pick up the fuller one later.

## Disclosure Order and the Reaction-as-Test Principle

**The actual rule, not derived from a prior failure but from direct discussion:** when a trans character is developing romantic or close interest in someone whose stance toward trans people isn't yet established, her own disclosure — if and when she chooses to share it — functions as a real, load-bearing trust test, not incidental texture. His reaction is diagnostic: a genuine, unhedged good reaction is a necessary condition before she would extend further trust, including any insight or suspicion she might independently have about him (such as reading him as an unaware egg himself, per the Recognition and Disclosure Pacing rule above). A poor or hedging reaction closes that door — she has no reason to extend further trust to someone who's just shown her he isn't safe to. **Sequencing matters concretely:** her disclosure and his reaction to it should be resolved *before* any progress on a separate thread where she might see something in him — the story shouldn't skip this order or treat the two threads as independent of each other, since in practice they aren't.

## A Character's Narrative Roles Are Separable, Not Bundled

**The actual rule:** a character who begins a story in a romantic role can also carry a separate, independent narrative function — mentor, anchor, trusted confidant, the person who helped another character through something real — and that second function does not require the romantic thread to continue or succeed in order to remain true. Stories default to assuming these are locked together simply because they started together; they aren't. A relationship can end, change shape, or never fully form while the character's other narrative role survives entirely intact. This matters specifically for trans characters given the Trans dating realities data above: a character shouldn't be written as narratively meaningful only for as long as she's also the love interest, since that would quietly reduce her value in the story to the romance alone.

## Co-op Membership vs. Residency — Not the Same Thing

**The actual rule:** a Household record (St_Claire_04_Households.md) makes someone a resident — this hasn't changed. **Co-op membership is a separate, narrower status requiring legal adulthood (18, real legal adulthood — not the community's drinking-age norm) and is never automatic** — it requires application, Council approval (associate membership: real benefit access, no vote, no leadership eligibility), then genuine tenure, demonstrated contribution, and sponsorship during the associate period itself, culminating in a ceremonial completion (full membership). Induction is universal and identity-blind — the same process regardless of who the applicant is. A rare denial carries a 90-day temporary safety-net bridge, not an immediate cutoff. Full process detail lives in St_Claire_00_Concept.md, Rites of Passage. Children are residents from birth, accessing all community resources as dependents under their household's membership, but do not hold independent membership themselves. This same 18-year threshold governs when any pull mechanic (Magic/Unrealism, St_Claire_00_Concept.md) can first activate for a given person — a child cannot be an egg, cannot receive the inverse pull, and cannot access the voluntary-transformation mechanic before adulthood, regardless of any other circumstance in a story.

**Marriage recognized by the Co-op (including triad/quad commitment) is real and binding for internal purposes only** — household records, Co-op benefits, safety net eligibility, community inheritance — and carries no automatic force outside St. Claire's boundary, the same real limitation tribal court marriage recognition has outside its own jurisdiction. This is the actual reason the contract/trust/healthcare-directive apparatus under Rites of Passage exists — it extends specific protections into the external world where Co-op recognition alone doesn't reach, not merely a workaround improvised by clever individual lawyers.

## Saturday Brunch Rotation

**The actual mechanic:** households rotate biweekly (Week A / Week B) through a set of six real breakfast-and-lunch venues (The Sunrise Counter, The Daily Grind, L'Aura, The Bindery, Hearthside Bakery, The Crimson Kettle), staggered across four two-hour time slots starting at 8:00 AM (8:00, 10:00, 12:00, 2:00). Every household's specific slot is recorded in its own record in St_Claire_04_Households.md — a new household needs one assigned, not left blank.

## Support While Questioning — Label vs. Help Are Not the Same Caution

**Failure this rule exists to prevent:** a scene withheld both a label and concrete help from a character questioning their identity, treating the two as the same kind of appropriate caution. They aren't — withholding a label ("I can't tell you who you are") protects someone's own agency over their identity; withholding concrete help ("here's something real to try tonight") when it's actually asked for just makes them push repeatedly for something that should have been offered immediately, dressed up as care.

**The actual rule:** the moment someone questioning asks for real, actionable suggestions, they get them immediately and specifically — not another round of gentle hedging first, not made to ask twice, not made to argue for their own agency before receiving it. Concretely: real, small, low-stakes things to actually try (a name, a pronoun spoken once alone, one piece of clothing) get offered the first time they're asked for, alongside the honest caveat that nobody else gets to decide who they are — those two things sit together easily and are never actually in tension. This doesn't have to wait to be asked, either: a community member who notices someone questioning can gently offer — "would you like some suggestions for things to try?" — genuine warmth and concern, not clinical distance, not just watching and waiting.

## Co-op Governance Decisions Are Binding, Not Just Provisionally Tolerated

**The actual rule:** any Co-op decision made through the correct venue (the monthly standing meeting, or Genesis Day where applicable) is treated as genuinely binding — the community actually respects an outcome it disagreed with, not merely complies with it while working to undermine it. Nothing is ever permanently closed; any member retains the real right to relitigate a settled question later, but only by bringing it back through that same correct process. Individual defiance or simply refusing to comply with a binding decision is never a legitimate way to reopen it — a story where a character disagrees with Co-op policy should show them working to actually change it through the process, not just ignoring it.

## Pull Mechanics Are Mutually Exclusive, Not a Shared Toolkit

Concept.md establishes two separate pull mechanics for a male visitor, built to produce opposite outcomes: the unaware-egg pull (Magic/Unrealism — draws a not-yet-aware trans woman toward St. Claire, resolves toward staying/becoming) and the inverse pull (draws a cis man who stays a cis man toward genuinely, warmly leaving — "not his to inhabit," a guest recognizing a boundary). They share surface texture (an unexplained draw toward the district, residents sensing something before it's named) because they're both "felt pull, not explained mechanism" — which makes them easy to blend by accident, not because they're actually compatible.

**Failure this rule exists to prevent:** a scene using the unaware-egg mechanic pulled in framing from the inverse mechanic (hospitality-to-a-guest, "not his to stay," gentle outward guidance) even though the story's actual premise required the opposite outcome — being drawn in and staying, not being gently walked back out. The two mechanics got treated as one shared "St. Claire has feelings about men" toolkit instead of two distinct, opposite-outcome tools.

**The actual rule:** before writing any scene using either pull mechanic, state explicitly which one is in play and what outcome it's building toward. The two never blend in a single scene — no gentle-guest framing in an egg story, no recognition-of-kinship framing in a story about someone who's staying cis. If a story is genuinely ambiguous about which one is happening (a real, valid premise), that ambiguity itself must be a deliberate, stated choice — not the accidental result of pulling language from both at once without noticing.

## Scene Setting Verification (hours, zone, and geography — not just people)

**Failure this rule exists to prevent:** Character Verification in Scenes (below) requires checking Population.md before any named character appears — but nothing required the equivalent check for the place itself. A scene was set at a real business during hours it doesn't operate (a 6:00 AM–2:00 PM diner, entered in the evening) and, separately, moved between zones without checking which zone a location actually belongs to. Both errors were checkable in seconds against Organizations.md and Places.md, but no rule required the check, so it didn't happen until directly caught.

**The actual rule:** before setting any scene at a named location, check Organizations.md for its actual operating hours (does this business exist and operate at the time the scene is set) and Places.md for its actual zone (is the geography — what's nearby, what zone it's in, how it connects to other locations already in the scene — consistent with what's on record). This runs the same way Character Verification does: before the scene is written, not after an error surfaces.

## Point-of-View Discipline for In-World Terminology

**Failure this rule exists to prevent:** the Womb-Based Street & Trail Network's own governing note states the contrast between organic and grid zones "is itself the point" and should be *felt*, physically, by whoever's moving through it — not explained. But a POV character with no reason to know Alcalá's planning vocabulary was at risk of having that vocabulary (Petal Roads, Circulatory Rings, Inner Chambers, and similar) narrated through him directly, which turns a felt physical contrast into exposition a character in his position wouldn't actually have access to.

**The actual rule:** in-world technical/lore terminology only appears in a scene through a character who would plausibly know and use it (residents, especially older ones or anyone connected to planning/civic life). A POV character without that knowledge experiences the same real contrast as unexplained physical sensation — a street that curves when it shouldn't, a path that feels used rather than built, disorientation without vocabulary for why — never as narrated terminology standing in for that sensation.

## Staffing Level Verification — real, sourced benchmarks, not eyeballed judgment

**Failure this rule exists to prevent:** no methodology existed anywhere for judging whether a business's listed "Staffing structure" was actually adequate for its stated size. A first attempt to fix this wrote a set of category benchmarks from memory — plausible-sounding numbers with no actual source — and presented them with the same confidence as the demographic ratios elsewhere in this document, which genuinely are sourced (Census, Harris Interactive, Autostraddle). That was the same failure this document exists to prevent, just relocated: assertion dressed up as verified fact. The numbers below were checked against real sources before being written in.

**Full-service dining (sit-down, waitstaff-served) — sourced from named industry operations guides (Nestlé Professional food-service staffing guidelines, via 7shifts; Shifty's restaurant staffing calculator methodology; role breakdowns from OpenTable, Toast, Lightspeed, and StaffedUp restaurant-position guides):**

**Failure this sub-rule exists to prevent:** the first version of this rule only checked server-to-seat headcount, and every business "fixed" under it (River Mill Eatery, L'Aura, Amour Noir) only got additional servers. A follow-up question exposed that none of the 38 Food & Hospitality businesses in Organizations.md have a named busser, dishwasher, host, or prep cook anywhere — a 40-seat diner with 4 total staff was being treated as adequately staffed by the seat-ratio check alone, while nobody who clears a table, washes a dish, or greets a guest existed in the record at all. Checking one ratio and declaring the category solved was the same failure this whole rule exists to prevent, one level deeper.

**The actual rule:** a full-service restaurant needs real coverage across both real functional groups, not just a headcount hitting a seat ratio:
- **Front-of-house:** host/greeter (can be absorbed into another role at small scale), server(s) sized to the seat ratio below, busser(s) for anything beyond the smallest rooms, bartender if there's a bar program.
- **Back-of-house:** a chef/cook, and — critically, the piece most often missing — dishwashing coverage. At small scale (roughly under 25–30 seats, modest menu), dishwashing and bussing can realistically be absorbed into existing roles during slower stretches rather than needing dedicated staff; this is a real, common small-restaurant pattern and shouldn't be treated as a violation on its own. Above that scale, or for any business with real volume, dedicated bussing and dishwashing coverage is expected, not optional.
- Server-to-seat ratios: casual/full-service 1 server per 4–6 tables (roughly 1 per 16–24 seats at a 4-top average); fine dining / high-touch / reservations-paced service 1 server per 3–4 tables (roughly 1 per 12–16 seats).
- Back-of-house kitchen sizing (cooks specifically) typically runs 60–80% of front-of-house headcount in full-service restaurants (source: Shifty).
- Counter-service, diner-style, and bar/taproom models are exempt from the seat ratio above — genuinely different service logic — but are not exempt from needing dishwashing coverage to exist somewhere in the record, even if it's absorbed into an existing named role rather than a dedicated position.

**Retail (storefronts, shops) — sourced from a named municipal planning study (Montgomery County / Metropolitan Washington Council of Governments, "Estimating Number of Employees in a Building," Round 7.0 employment-density conversion factors):** that study gives two real figures — roughly 1.1 employees per 1,000 square feet, and a separate conversion factor of 450 square feet per employee — meaning real sourced figures for the same underlying question don't fully agree with each other, which is itself worth stating rather than hiding: retail staffing density is genuinely less standardized than dining. Since floor square footage isn't tracked in Places.md for most storefronts, the practical translation: a small single-room storefront (roughly the size of the district's typical named shops) plausibly supports 1–3 people; a sole proprietor with occasional help is realistic and common, not automatically understaffed.

**Health & wellness (clinics, practices) — sourced from primary-care workforce research (HRSA/AAMC-cited panel-size literature, and a 2016 Journal of the American Board of Family Medicine review specifically examining whether standard panel-size figures hold up):** commonly cited primary-care panel sizes run in the 1,200–2,500 patient-per-physician range, though that same review is explicit that the often-quoted 2,500 figure "arises in the literature anecdotally, without a basis in research," and that panel size varies heavily with team-based task delegation (nurses, techs absorbing work) and patient complexity. Given that real, sourced uncertainty, a small or solo practice (like St. Claire Counseling's sole therapist, or the veterinary hospital's two-person model) shouldn't be treated as a violation on headcount alone — the real check is whether staff composition (is there support staff absorbing volume, or is one person doing everything) matches the described patient/client volume and community role.

**Civic/nonprofit institutions:** no clean sourced ratio exists for this category the way it does for dining, retail, or primary care — searched for one and found none worth citing as a real standard. This gets checked by comparison against other, already-built St. Claire institutions of similar scale and mission, with the reasoning shown, and stated explicitly as a judgment call rather than dressed up with numerical precision it doesn't have.

**Trades & industrial:** scales with real, checkable logic already available elsewhere in this document (bay count, crew size, project volume) rather than needing a new ratio of its own.

**When a real ratio applies and a business fails it,** the fix is the same as any other confirmed error: add the missing role as a real, complete character (full Population record, no thin placeholder), the same rigor as any other new addition — not a note left for later.

## Character Verification in Scenes (not just staffing lookups)

**Failure this rule exists to prevent:** during creative writing, every named character who filled an *employee* role in a scene got correctly checked against Organizations.md staffing lists first — that check was reliable. But two characters who weren't anyone's employee (a bar patron, an outdoor market vendor) got invented on the spot with zero check at all, because the only verification habit that existed was "look up who works here," and neither of them worked anywhere. The gap wasn't carelessness on those two specifically — it was that no check existed for "anyone appearing in a scene who isn't staff."

**The actual rule, going forward:** before any named character appears in a scene — staff, patron, passerby, anyone with a name and a line of dialogue — check Population.md first, not just Organizations.md. If the character type doesn't map to an obvious lookup (an employee list), that's not a reason to skip the check; it's a reason to search Population.md more broadly (by role, by location, by whatever's plausible) before deciding no one fits.

**If no existing character fits:** say so, and ask whether to build a new one before using them in the scene — the same real process as any other new addition (name-collision check, full field set, household, no "not yet determined" left behind). Do not invent a name inline and continue the scene, planning to formalize them "later." Later is exactly when it stops happening, which is what happened tonight.

## Propose Before Building — for anything not explicitly requested

**Failure this rule exists to prevent:** on more than one occasion, a full section of new content (infrastructure systems, policy decisions, mechanics) got written directly into the documents without being asked for first — presented as a finished addition rather than a proposal. This happened even after being corrected for it once already in the same session, which means "try to remember not to do this" is not a sufficient fix on its own.

**The actual rule:** if a piece of content wasn't explicitly requested — a new system, a new policy, a new mechanism, a new named institution beyond what's needed to answer the specific question asked — it gets proposed in conversation first, and only written into any document after real confirmation. This applies regardless of how confident the reasoning feels in the moment, and regardless of how small the addition seems. "I was going to ask anyway" is not the same as asking.

## Structural Integrity Check After Any Large Edit

**Failure this rule exists to prevent:** a section header was fully deleted during a str_replace operation on a section boundary — twice, the same exact mistake both times — because the old and new text blocks didn't line up cleanly and a heading got silently absorbed into the replacement.

**The actual rule:** after any str_replace or edit that touches a section boundary (anywhere a `##` heading sits near the edited text), re-view or re-grep the document's full header structure (`grep -n "^## "`) before considering the edit complete. This check is mandatory for large edits specifically — the kind that span multiple paragraphs or cross a heading — not for small, single-line changes where the risk doesn't really exist.

## Household Label Uniqueness

**Failure this rule exists to prevent:** single-person household labels were generated using first-name-only ("Sandra (single) Household"), which silently collided whenever multiple people shared a first name — 39 separate collisions, 229 affected entries, discovered only during a full audit rather than at creation time.

**The actual rule:** every new single-person household label uses the person's full name ("Sandra Kowalczyk (single) Household"), never first name alone. For multi-person households, the existing hyphenated-surname or informal-house-name convention (see Households template) already avoids this problem and doesn't need to change.

## Physical Description Completeness

**Failure this rule exists to prevent:** 45% of the adult population (178 of 396) had physical descriptions reading as little as "Femme. White. Average height." — categorically complete but genuinely unusable in a scene. A further 33 people had rich occupational/style detail but zero hair reference at all. Both gaps went undetected until a direct complaint about generic-feeling characters in an actual scene.

**The actual rule:** every physical description must include, at minimum, presentation, ethnicity, a real hair color/style detail, and a real clothing or style detail — not "preferred," mandatory. "Preferred" language let 312 of 396 people through with zero clothing anchor the first time this rule existed in weaker form. This is checked the same way demographic ratios are — before a new batch is considered finished, not after a complaint surfaces.

## Prose Introduction Completeness (distinct from the Population.md record rule above)

**Failure this rule exists to prevent:** two characters carried a full scene of dialogue and real chemistry with incomplete physical grounding — present were hair, eyes, and clothing (partial), but both were missing skin tone, hands, and any description of how they actually sound, and neither gap was noticed until directly asked whether the description was complete enough to picture them.

**The actual rule, mandatory core only — small on purpose, not a full checklist:** a character's first real on-page appearance in a scene needs, at minimum: an approximate age/build/height impression, hair, a skin tone or ethnicity marker, general clothing (silhouette, not itemized head-to-toe), and — specifically for any character carrying significant dialogue in the scene — some sense of how they actually sound (pitch, pace, accent, cadence), since dialogue-heavy characters are otherwise fully audible and invisible at once. One genuinely distinguishing, specific detail (not generic) rounds this out.

**Explicitly not mandatory, to avoid mechanical checklist-prose:** footwear, hands, scent, nail detail, facial hair status, and similar texture — these enrich a description when they fit naturally, but requiring all of them for every character produces itemized inventory instead of writing. The line is: enough to picture the person, not an inventory of the person.

## Men in the Population — Residents vs. Visitors

**No named male business owners, and no named male residents beyond confirmed trans men.** Every apparent case found in the population turned out to be the naming bug above — a woman incorrectly given a masculine first name — and once corrected, zero remain. This isn't an accident to leave as-is; it's now the actual rule. St. Claire's core identity is lesbian, and that stays true at the population level, not just as a stated value undercut by who's actually named in the roster.

**The one established exception stays an exception, not a loophole:** dedicated male counselors for boys (see Concept.md, Raising Children) remain real and unnamed, filling a specific functional need. This doesn't open the door to named male staff, owners, or residents elsewhere — it's a single, deliberately scoped role, not a precedent.

**Visitors are a genuinely different category, and don't need this rule applied to them.** St. Claire is an established destination district pulling 10,000-40,000 weekly visitors — people passing through, not residents. A cis man showing up as a visitor, tourist, or someone who took a wrong exit is consistent with the world as already built, and doesn't require him to become a named Population.md entry with a household and an occupation, because he isn't part of the community's actual population. He's real for the length of a story and doesn't need to be tracked as a resident unless a story makes him one.

**The concrete line, so "visitor" can't quietly stretch into indefinite residency:** a household record is what makes someone a resident, nothing softer than that. Visitors stay in hotel infrastructure (the budget motel through the Riverstone Grand) — genuinely temporary lodging by design, not housing stock. A man can be a guest at a hotel for the length of a story. The moment a story would require him to have an actual household — a fixed residence rather than a hotel room — that's the exact point "visitor" stops applying, and it becomes the same real exception described next, requiring genuine discussion first rather than something a story can drift into by inertia.

**Membership is closed to adult cis men with exactly two narrow exceptions, not a general discretionary exception process — see St_Claire_00_Concept.md, Governance, for full detail.** A man cannot gain membership through arrival, marriage, or contribution regardless of circumstance. The only two paths: raised in St. Claire as a son from birth, reaching 18 through the normal membership process; or the rare voluntary-transformation mechanic (Magic/Unrealism) for a man whose bond with a member is deep enough that separation would cause genuine, severe distress. This is an absolute baseline, not a default that a strong individual story case can argue around.

## Name/Presentation Consistency

**Failure this rule exists to prevent:** 48 people across the population had traditionally masculine first names (Robert, Michael, Brian, James, Kevin, Scott, and similar) paired with Femme, Butch, or Androgynous presentation and no "Trans man" tag — several with explicit "her" pronoun usage directly contradicting the name. This came from bulk-generation batches pulling first names from an ungated shared pool instead of one matched to gender presentation, and went undetected until direct complaint about a specific character.

**The actual rule:** first names must match established presentation. Traditionally masculine first names are reserved specifically for characters tagged "Trans man" — no exceptions elsewhere, including Androgynous-tagged characters, unless explicitly built as a deliberate exception with real discussion first (see Propose Before Building, above). This check runs the same way demographic ratios do — before a batch is considered finished, not after.

**Names are drawn from St_Claire_08_Name_Pools.md, not invented freely.** The same "reference, don't invent" discipline already established for Occupations (St_Claire_07) applies to names: check the pool, matched to presentation and — where age is known — generation, before naming a new character. This exists specifically to prevent the surname-repetition and celebrity-echo problems documented in that file's own source material, and to keep the population's naming texture consistent rather than drifting batch to batch. Specific checks: no surname appears more than twice in the population unless it's an intentional, documented family relationship; no celebrity-name echoes, real or fictional-famous (the same principle that produced the "Vance" naming-gimmick cut elsewhere in this project); surnames already heavily used in Population.md should be treated as at-capacity — check current usage before adding another.

## Local Slang & Language

**No catalogued glossary, deliberately.** Local shorthand grows organically out of inside jokes and gentle ribbing — most of it is lived, unrecorded tradition, and inventing specific examples in advance manufactures texture instead of letting it emerge when a story actually needs a piece of it.

**"Community Girl Talk" is one deliberate, named exception** — real, institutional-weight slang, not just organic texture. Used literally in conversation ("this needs some Girl Talk," "that was a real Girl Talk moment").

**"Egg" is the second deliberate, named exception** — real, existing trans-community slang (not St. Claire-invented), referring to a trans woman who hasn't yet realized/"hatched" into her identity. In-world, St. Claire residents use it exactly the way the real trans community does: sometimes affectionately among themselves about someone they suspect, sometimes as gentle self-directed humor after the fact ("I was such an egg"), sometimes as a lower-stakes, first way of raising the subject before the direct conversation happens.

**How "egg" relates to the Direct Naming rule above — this is the important part, not a separate concern:** "egg" is allowed as real, textured dialogue — residents can say it to each other, joke with it, use it as a first, softer approach. But it never substitutes for the actual moment the Direct Naming rule requires. If a resident uses "egg" *to* the character in question as a stand-in for ever saying "transgender" outright — a wink instead of a word — that's the exact same hedged-avoidance failure the rule above exists to prevent, just wearing community slang instead of clinical euphemism. The real word still has to land on the page. "Egg" can open the door or season the scene around it; it can't be the whole conversation.

## Story-Craft Content Rules

Checkable content rules, moved out of Concept.md's Thematic Values section per the standing extraction rule (see Governing Process Rules, above) — Concept.md keeps the why; these are the actual checkable rules that reasoning produces. Every story set in St. Claire is checked against these the same way a scene is checked against Character Verification or Scene Setting Verification.

- **Polyamory is portrayed as emotional, not erotic.**
- **Femininity is written as collaborative, not competitive** — shapes how women and femme characters relate to each other throughout a scene, not incidental flavor.
- **Domination dynamics are avoided.** Relationships and conflict aren't built on power-over dynamics.
- **Degradation, humiliation, and "sissy"-genre content are no-gos.** These frame femininity as inherently lesser or shameful, which directly contradicts the collaborative-not-competitive rule above. Much of that genre is abusive in practice, not just in tone.
- **Forced feminization by a partner is a no-go** (it's an abuse dynamic); **forced feminization by an external force is a legitimate plot device.** The distinction is who's imposing it — external coercion (legal, corporate, circumstantial) is fair territory, a partner imposing it on another is not.
- **Infidelity is used sparingly, if at all.** When it appears, it's a catalyst for real change, always genuinely damaging to trust, and may reasonably end a relationship rather than being smoothed over.
- **Desire toward trans women is written as ordinary and legitimate, not hedged or narratively justified.** This sits alongside the existing hypocrisy-test in Concept.md's Setting section (a community that embraces strap-ons has no consistent basis for treating trans anatomy as disqualifying) — that test catches bad-faith discomfort; this rule covers the positive case the project never actually stated: genuine attraction to a trans woman doesn't need special handling or explanation to be written as real.
- **Bodily mortification is met with simple, practical grace, not a correction spiral.** A character's discomfort with an involuntary bodily response is real and can be felt on the page, but the other character's response should be brief and kind — an offer of privacy, a light unbothered word — not extended dialogue processing the moment into a scene of reassurance. The warmth lives in the gesture, not in paragraphs proving the gesture was kind.

## Sisterprise — recognizing the pattern

An institution qualifies as a "Sisterprise" when it combines genuine individual entrepreneurial drive with a structure that directs real surplus back to the collective rather than accumulating as private wealth. Not every business qualifies — check for both halves (real entrepreneurial origin, real collective-benefit structure) before applying the label.
