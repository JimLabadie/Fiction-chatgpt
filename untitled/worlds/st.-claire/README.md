## Concept baseline

St. Claire is a reusable lesbian community designed to support many stories: a standing well of recurring people, institutions, businesses, and settings without requiring every story to rebuild its world.

Population is roughly **14,500**, deliberately mid-size: large enough for neighborhood variety and new people, small enough for recurring paths to cross plausibly.

St. Claire is a distinct district adjacent to or within a larger unnamed urban area, not an isolated standalone town. It is a destination whose restaurants, nightlife, museums, and culture draw outsiders as well as residents.

The lesbian identity is the center of gravity. Trans lesbians are included without qualification. Other sexualities and gender identities can be present and treated with respect without displacing that central identity.

Individual St. Claire stories may use unrealistic or magical elements, often gender-related, but those are **story-level mechanics rather than a fixed community-wide magic system**. A story may bend the world; the district itself should remain solid enough that the bending has something real to push against.

Household economics can produce above-average household affluence through multi-partner households and generally lower child-rearing costs, while still preserving meaningful economic variation rather than making everyone independently wealthy.

St. Claire is a destination district whose density benchmark uses Park Slope as a real-world calibration point while deliberately retaining a Midwestern, car-dependent movement model. Business-density targets belong in rules/mechanics rather than being inferred ad hoc.

## Design Lens — EPCOT Taken Literally

St. Claire can be understood as **EPCOT taken literally**: an experimental community that treats ordinary civic systems as things that can be continually reconsidered rather than inherited unchanged.

Much of St. Claire’s apparent futurism is deliberately **not invented technology**. Its infrastructure and urban design draw heavily from real systems, historical examples, engineering projects, documentaries, cities, institutions, and technologies encountered during development. The creative act is often in recognizing that an existing solution to one problem can be combined with other proven ideas and applied more comprehensively.

The recurring St. Claire pattern is:

**ordinary problem → question the inherited assumption → find something already proven somewhere → connect it to other useful systems → implement it thoroughly → make the resulting human experience pleasantly ordinary.**

This produces what can be described as **defensible lunacy**: individual components may be real, practical, or historically demonstrated, while their integration and commitment at community scale can become gloriously excessive.

Technology is not meant to be the spectacle. **The life it enables is the point.**

St. Claire also does not treat aesthetics as an afterthought. Serious infrastructure can be beautiful, playful, culturally lesbian, irreverent, or occasionally a deliberate middle finger. Functionality does not require sterility.

When recovering or developing St. Claire material, **do not flatten detailed mechanisms into generic outcomes**. Waste processing is not merely “sustainability”; transit is not merely “good public transportation”; urban planning is not merely “walkability.” The mechanisms, interactions, economics, aesthetics, cultural choices, and reasons the systems exist are part of the worldbuilding.

Many original concepts were accumulated through years of encountering real-world technologies and historical examples, then refined through discussion. Source documents may therefore contain important connections whose significance is not obvious in isolation. Preserve those connections during recovery rather than summarizing first and attempting to reconstruct them later.
