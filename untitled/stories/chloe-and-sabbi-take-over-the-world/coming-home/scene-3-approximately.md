# Scene 3 — Approximately

There are moments in a woman's life when she is required to make a choice.

Career.

Marriage.

Children.

Whether putting twenty million dollars on her feet counts as getting dressed or committing insurance fraud.

Sabbi sat on a bench and stared at the Moon Shoes.

“I've changed my mind.”

“Okay.”

She looked at me.

That apparently wasn't the answer she'd expected.

“Okay?”

“Okay.”

“You're not going to convince me?”

“Nope.”

“Tell me I'll regret it?”

“Nope.”

“Point out that I may never get another opportunity?”

“They live here.”

She looked at the shoes.

Right.

That was apparently still sinking in.

“They live here.”

“Yep.”

“In our house.”

I smiled.

“In our house.”

She looked at me suspiciously.

“You're enjoying this.”

“Immensely.”

“Not the shoes.”

“No.”

“The pronouns.”

“Immensely.”

She leaned back and stared at the ceiling.

“I hate you.”

Athena said, “Available evidence suggests otherwise.”

Sabbi pointed upward.

“Stay out of this.”

“I live here.”

“Oh my God, she did it to me.”

I laughed.

Sabbi looked back at the shoes.

Then sighed.

“No. Fuck it. I'm doing it.”

“Excellent.”

“But if I fall—”

“I'll catch you.”

“If you fall while catching me?”

“Athena will erase the security footage.”

“I will not.”

“See?” I said. “Family.”

Sabbi slipped off her shoes.

Then stopped.

“Wait.”

“What?”

“What size are these?”

I looked at the Moon Shoes.

Then at her feet.

Then upward.

“Athena?”

“The original Moon Star Shoes were constructed for their intended wearer.”

Sabbi stared at me.

I stared upward.

“That was aggressively unhelpful.”

“It was accurate.”

“Can she wear them?”

“Not safely as currently configured.”

Sabbi sagged with what looked suspiciously like relief.

“Oh, thank God.”

“However—”

“No.”

Athena continued.

“—the Estate's conservation and fitting systems can determine whether a non-destructive temporary fit accommodation is possible.”

Sabbi looked horrified.

“You have a machine for making museum shoes fit?”

“No,” I said.

A beat.

“Probably.”

“Chloe.”

“This place surprises me too.”

“That is not reassuring.”

A nearby system quietly activated.

Sabbi looked at it.

Then at me.

“No cutting.”

“Obviously.”

“No glue.”

“Obviously.”

“No drilling twenty-million-dollar shoes.”

“Sabbi.”

“I need to hear you say it.”

“We will not drill the Moon Shoes.”

“Thank you.”

Athena said, “That would also violate conservation protocol.”

“See?” I said.

Sabbi pointed at the ceiling.

“She's the responsible one.”

“I know. It's embarrassing.”

The system did whatever incomprehensible things the system did.

We waited.

Sabbi swung her bare feet.

I sat beside her.

For perhaps thirty seconds, neither of us said anything.

Then she nudged me with her shoulder.

“You really meant it?”

“What?”

“All this.”

I looked at her.

She gestured vaguely.

The shoes.

The wardrobe.

The Estate.

Possibly the entire square footage of our increasingly questionable life.

“Yeah.”

“You just decided I live here?”

“No.”

Her face changed.

I nudged her back.

“I decided you _could_ live here.”

“Oh.”

“You decided the rest.”

She looked down at her hands.

Simple.

That was how I wanted this part.

No speeches.

No grand declaration about belonging.

Just the truth.

After a moment, Sabbi nodded toward the Moon Shoes.

“I'm still wearing those fuckers.”

“There she is.”

The system chimed.

Athena said, “A reversible fitting solution is available. No alteration to the shoes is required.”

Sabbi stood.

“Let's make terrible choices.”

“That is the spirit of this household.”

“I heard that,” Athena said.

“You were supposed to.”

The fitting itself was almost disappointingly civilized.

No lasers.

No robotic arms descending dramatically from the ceiling.

No tiny conservator bursting through a wall screaming _absolutely not_.

The shoes were prepared.

Sabbi sat.

I crouched in front of her.

She immediately pulled her feet away.

“What are you doing?”

“Helping.”

“You are not putting twenty-million-dollar shoes on me.”

“Why?”

“Because that's weird.”

“It's shoes, not a coronation.”

“With these shoes, I'm not convinced.”

“Give me your foot.”

“No.”

“Sabbi.”

“Chloe.”

“Foot.”

She gave me her foot.

“I'm only doing this because I want to see them.”

“Obviously.”

“And because they're ridiculous.”

“Obviously.”

“And because I have made an informed adult decision.”

“Obviously.”

“And not because you're bossy.”

I looked up at her.

She looked down at me.

“Would you like your foot back?”

“No.”

I slipped the first shoe on.

Sabbi stopped talking.

Well.

There was a first time for everything.

I fastened it carefully.

Then the other.

I sat back on my heels.

She stared at her feet.

The Moon Shoes sparkled.

They were absurd.

They were excessive.

They were an argument against civilization.

They were also fucking spectacular.

“Oh,” Sabbi said.

“Yeah.”

“Oh.”

“Still want them off?”

“No.”

I stood and offered both hands.

“Then up.”

She took them.

Slowly, she stood.

Her eyes went wide.

“I am wearing a house.”

“Several houses.”

“Don't.”

“Approximately.”

Her mouth fell open.

From somewhere above us:

“I approve.”

Sabbi started laughing.

Not polite laughter.

Not cute laughter.

The kind that takes your knees out.

She grabbed my shoulders.

“You bitch.”

“You started it.”

“You said approximately.”

“I learned from my daughter.”

“I hate this family.”

Athena said, “Available evidence—”

“ATHENA!”

Sabbi laughed harder.

And that was how the Moon Shoes almost killed her.

Not financially.

Not physically.

Comedically.

Eventually she straightened.

“Okay.”

“You good?”

“I think so.”

“Walk?”

Her expression changed.

“Oh.”

Right.

Twenty million dollars had not purchased immunity from gravity.

I offered my arm.

Sabbi looked at it.

Then at me.

Then she took it.

“One step.”

She stepped.

Nothing exploded.

Another.

Then another.

Her posture changed.

Shoulders back.

Chin up.

Not because the shoes transformed her.

Because she had stopped being afraid of them.

“There you go.”

“Don't patronize me. I'm carrying the GDP of a small island nation.”

“Approximately.”

She elbowed me.

We made it out of the secure area.

Then through the wardrobe.

Slowly.

Very slowly.

Sabbi looked down every few steps.

“Stop watching your feet.”

“They're covered in diamonds.”

“Your feet?”

“You know what I mean.”

“You'll walk better if you look ahead.”

“How do you know?”

“I own a lot of heels.”

“An irresponsible number of heels.”

“Yes.”

“A morally questionable number.”

“Yes.”

“A number that should cause a Senate hearing.”

“Probably.”

She looked ahead.

Another step.

Then another.

Better.

“Oh.”

“See?”

“Don't get smug.”

“Wouldn't dream of it.”

We reached the main corridor.

Sabbi stopped.

“What now?”

I looked left.

Then right.

That was an excellent question.

We were home.

We had no itinerary.

No mission.

No reason to do anything.

I smiled.

“What do you want to do?”

Sabbi considered this with the seriousness of a woman wearing twenty-million-dollar shoes in a converted shopping mall owned by her best friend.

Then she looked toward the door Athena had opened earlier.

“The spa.”

I burst out laughing.

“You remembered?”

“Chloe, I am a lesbian. We remember resources.”

“Athena?”

“Yes, Mom?”

“Is the spa available?”

“Yes.”

Sabbi squeezed my arm.

“Bestie.”

“Yes?”

“I have a proposal.”

“Oh God.”

“I would like to go to our spa—”

I grinned at _our_.

She saw it.

“Don't.”

I said nothing.

“—while wearing the Moon Shoes.”

“That seems deeply impractical.”

“Thank you.”

“And then?”

She thought about it.

“I don't know.”

There it was.

I liked that answer.

I slipped my arm through hers.

“Perfect.”

We started walking.

Carefully.

Very carefully.

Behind us, the secure case stood empty.

Ahead of us was absolutely no plan whatsoever.

For the first time all day, that felt exactly right.

Sabbi made it another six careful steps.

Then she stopped.

“Wait.”

“What?”

She turned as much as twenty-million-dollar shoes permitted and looked back toward the Grand Wardrobe.

“Bestie.”

“Yes?”

“How many?”

I blinked.

“How many what?”

She pointed behind us.

“All of it.”

“That is not a useful unit of measurement.”

“Clothes. Shoes. Purses. Jewelry. All the girly shit.”

“Oh.”

“Yes, _oh._”

I looked upward.

“Athena?”

There was a pause.

Sabbi's eyes narrowed.

“Why did she pause?”

“I don't know.”

“Chloe.”

“I genuinely don't know.”

“Athena,” Sabbi said carefully, “how many things are in our wardrobe?”

And now I wanted to know too.
