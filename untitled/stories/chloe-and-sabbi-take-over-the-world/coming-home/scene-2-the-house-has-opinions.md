# Scene 2 — The House Has Opinions

“Okay,” Sabbi said.

We had made it twelve feet.

Possibly fifteen.

Progress was subjective in a house where your best friend kept stopping every four seconds.

“Okay what?”

She pointed behind us.

“That was a mall.”

“Technically, I believe it still is a mall.”

“You live in a mall.”

“We live in a mall.”

“Stop doing that.”

“Doing what?”

“Changing pronouns and thereby altering my socioeconomic status.”

I considered this.

“Would you prefer _our mall_?”

Sabbi stared at me.

Athena said, “I like her.”

“Stay out of this.”

“I live here.”

Sabbi looked upward.

“See, that's a valid argument.”

“Do not team up with my daughter.”

“Your daughter has excellent instincts.”

“My daughter bought Mattel because she wanted a Barbie submarine.”

Sabbi froze.

I closed my eyes.

Athena said nothing.

Which was how I knew she was guilty.

Slowly, Sabbi turned toward me.

“She what?”

“Nothing.”

“Chloe.”

“It isn't relevant.”

“Chloe.”

“Athena, distraction.”

A door opened to our left.

Sabbi looked.

I looked.

There was a pause.

Then Sabbi whispered, “Is that a spa?”

God, I loved my daughter.

“Yes.”

She walked toward it.

I caught her arm.

“Later.”

“You can't point a lesbian at a spa and say later.”

“I didn't point you anywhere. Athena opened a door.”

“Semantics.”

Athena said, “I can schedule—”

“No.”

“—anything you would like.”

Sabbi looked at me.

Then upward.

Then at me again.

“You've made a terrible mistake.”

“I am beginning to understand that.”

We kept walking.

The Estate unfolded around us without having the decency to become less ridiculous.

There were places that still carried the bones of the old Galleria beneath everything we'd done to it—impossible spans of open space, soaring ceilings, broad passages built for crowds. Except now those bones had been dressed by somebody who apparently believed Barbie deserved Versailles.

Which, to be fair, she did.

Sabbi walked beside me, looking everywhere.

I tried not to watch her looking everywhere.

That lasted maybe thirty seconds.

She caught me.

“What?”

“Nothing.”

“You're doing the face again.”

“I have many faces.”

“You have three. Innocent, guilty, and _wait until she sees this shit_.”

“That seems reductive.”

“Which one is this?”

I smiled.

She stopped.

“Oh, fuck you.”

“Come on.”

“No. Whatever is around that corner, I reject it in advance.”

“You haven't seen it.”

“I don't need to. I know your face now.”

I kept walking.

She followed.

Obviously.

The corridor opened.

Sabbi stopped.

Again.

In front of us was the Grand Wardrobe.

Calling it a closet would have been actionable defamation.

Sabbi took one step forward.

Then another.

She stared.

“Chloe.”

“Yes?”

“No.”

“Yes.”

“Is this…”

“Our wardrobe.”

She turned.

“Our.”

“There you go. You're learning.”

She walked farther inside.

The space stretched away in beautifully organized sections and levels, clothing and accessories disappearing beyond what any reasonable person could need in several lifetimes.

Fortunately, reasonable people did not live here.

Sabbi reached toward a dress.

Stopped before touching it.

Looked at me.

“Can I?”

That one got me.

Not because the question was sad.

Because it was sincere.

Because for all the jokes and all the ridiculousness, she still didn't quite believe me.

I stepped beside her.

“Bestie.”

She looked at me.

“You live here.”

Her eyes flicked toward the dress.

Then back to me.

“This isn't a museum where I'm going to get tackled by a conservator?”

“Well.”

Her hand snapped back.

I laughed.

“Some things actually are museum pieces. We have systems for that.”

“Of course you do.”

“But this?”

I touched the dress she'd been looking at.

“Ask the system.”

Sabbi looked upward.

“Athena?”

“Nope.”

She looked at me.

“The house has a second voice?”

“Several, probably.”

“Your house has departments?”

“My house has governance.”

“That is so much worse.”

A nearby display came quietly to life.

Sabbi stared at it.

Information appeared.

She read.

Then looked at me.

Then back at the display.

“It's telling me I can wear it.”

“Then you can wear it.”

She looked down at herself.

Then at the dress.

Then at me.

“What else can I wear?”

I smiled.

There was the question.

“Now you're getting it.”

She started walking.

Slowly at first.

Then faster.

The questions began almost immediately.

“This?”

“Ask.”

“This?”

“Ask.”

“Oh my God, this?”

“Ask.”

“Chloe.”

“What?”

“There are shoes.”

“Yes.”

“A lot of shoes.”

“Yes.”

“An irresponsible number of shoes.”

I said nothing.

She turned toward me.

“You're doing the face.”

“I don't know what you mean.”

“You have worse shoes somewhere.”

“I object to the word worse.”

“More expensive?”

“Possibly.”

“More ridiculous?”

“That is subjective.”

“More likely to require their own insurance policy?”

I started walking.

She followed instantly.

“Chloe.”

“Hmm?”

“Chloe.”

“I heard you.”

“Where are we going?”

“Nowhere.”

“Liar.”

“I resent how quickly you've become comfortable accusing me of things in my own home.”

“Our home.”

I stopped.

She grinned.

Oh.

Oh, that little bitch.

“Very good.”

“I'm learning.”

We reached the secure footwear area.

The system recognized me.

Then paused.

Sabbi looked at me.

I looked upward.

“Athena?”

“Yes, Mom?”

“Partner-equivalent household access.”

“Already propagated where your authority permits.”

Sabbi blinked.

“You did that already?”

Athena answered her.

“Mom asked.”

Something passed over Sabbi's face.

Small.

Quick.

Real.

Then the access indicator changed.

The door opened.

Sabbi looked inside.

She did not move.

I waited.

“Bestie?”

“No.”

I smiled.

“Good no or bad no?”

“Shut up.”

She walked in.

Rows of shoes surrounded us.

Pumps. Sandals. Boots. Platforms. Crystals. Satin. Suede. Patent leather. Objects that could reasonably be called footwear and objects that had clearly escaped from an argument between sculpture and gravity.

Sabbi turned in a slow circle.

Then she saw the secure display.

“Oh, those are pretty.”

I leaned against the doorway.

“They are.”

She stepped closer.

Very close.

“Chloe.”

“Yes?”

“Why are these in their own case?”

“No reason.”

She looked at me.

“The face.”

“I still maintain this face taxonomy is pseudoscience.”

She read the display.

Silence.

Then she read it again.

“Chloe.”

“Yes, bestie?”

“How much?”

I considered lying.

Athena said, “Approximately nineteen point nine million dollars.”

“ATHENA.”

“You appeared to be experiencing difficulty answering.”

Sabbi turned toward me.

Slowly.

“Twenty.”

“Approximately.”

“Million.”

“Approximately.”

“Dollars.”

“I feel we're getting repetitive.”

“For shoes.”

“They're very nice shoes.”

She looked back at them.

The Moon Shoes glittered peacefully inside their case, entirely untroubled by the economic questions raised by their existence.

Sabbi pressed both hands to her cheeks.

“Oh my God.”

“Would you like to wear them?”

Her hands dropped.

“What?”

I shrugged.

“Would you like to wear them?”

“Chloe, they're twenty million dollars.”

“That wasn't my question.”

“They're in a case.”

“They're shoes.”

“They're art.”

“They can be two things.”

“They have actual diamonds.”

“I believe that's part of why they cost twenty million dollars.”

“Approximately,” Athena added.

I pointed upward.

“You're on thin ice.”

“I have no physical mass.”

Sabbi laughed.

Then looked at the shoes again.

I watched her.

No pushing.

No speech.

No lesson.

Just the choice.

Finally she looked at me.

Her grin arrived slowly.

It was magnificent.

“Bestie.”

“Yes?”

“I want to wear the stupid shoes.”

I grinned back.

“Athena?”

“Yes, Mom?”

“Help us commit a terrible financial decision.”

“The shoes are already owned. Wearing them creates no new purchase.”

Sabbi looked at me.

I sighed.

“She does this.”

“I fucking love her.”

The case unlocked.

And somewhere in the Estate, I was reasonably certain common sense packed a small suitcase and left without saying goodbye.
