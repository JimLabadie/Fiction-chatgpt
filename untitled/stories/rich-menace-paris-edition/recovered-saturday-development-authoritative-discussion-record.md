## Saturday shape

Yes. That gives Saturday an actual shape instead of “telephone, time warp, dinner.” And the grocery shopping is particularly good because the denial can do comedy for us.

I see a really nice escalation:

1. Family calls first. They survive the parental reaction only to discover they’ve somehow acquired a joint family dinner that evening. This establishes the clock on the day without consuming it.
2. Grocery shopping because they absolutely are not a couple. They need food because Chloe is now living there. Except they’re standing in a grocery store discussing coffee, breakfast preferences, snacks, what Chloe cooks, what Allison actually eats, toiletries, household staples—basically performing Domestic Couple: The Musical while insisting this is practical infrastructure for a fake girlfriend. Better still, Chloe naturally starts learning Allison through the cart: what she reaches for, what she doesn’t buy, what she’s been living on while buried in work.
3. Home chores split according to who they actually are. Chloe sees cleaning and simply annexes it. Not because Allison can’t clean, but because Chloe genuinely enjoys domestic care and immediately has Opinions™ about doing it properly. Allison doesn’t fight for a mop to prove feminism happened. She happily takes the home-maintenance list—the little physical jobs that accumulate in an owned house. That lets both women contribute competently in different ways. And because Allison bought a family-sized Colonial while allegedly swearing off romance, there is enormous comic potential in Chloe discovering just how much maintenance her “definitely temporary fake girlfriend residence” requires.
4. Friends are where Saturday can really open up. Family has now entered their shared life; friends should start seeing the same ridiculous situation from a different angle. Allison already has six developed friends, whereas Chloe’s five are deliberately unnamed. That gives us an actual creative decision rather than something I should silently fill in.

And there’s a lovely mechanism available: the grocery store/chores don’t need to be separate from the friend introduction. One of Allison’s friends can call or drop by during Domestic Saturday and encounter Chloe not as “the fake girlfriend Allison hired yesterday,” but as a woman barefoot/in house clothes, putting groceries away or cleaning something while Allison is fixing a cabinet hinge.

That friend gets approximately eight seconds to think:

Oh.

Because Julie already knows. Family knows. But a friend seeing them in situ gives us an outside observer who can appreciate how catastrophically bad these two are at not being a couple.

My instinct is one or two friends, not the whole six-person squad yet. A full friend-group introduction becomes an Event. One close friend wandering into Saturday lets the relationship reveal itself accidentally—and gives us room to actually establish that friend as a person.

And I would keep Chloe’s friends for later. Meeting Allison’s world first gives Chloe somewhere new to enter; when Allison eventually meets Chloe’s people, we can make that reciprocal and meaningful instead of burning both introductions on the same Saturday.

## Nona and the roots of Chloe's hospitality

i agree i think we need a bit less beatifying, because its real chloe can talk about nona.

Yes. That fixes something important. I was starting to turn Chloe into a résumé of Perfect Wife Features instead of letting her be a person who simply likes doing this stuff.

Nona gives it roots.

Chloe doesn’t make little appetizers because she’s preternaturally attentive or because the universe issued her the Deluxe Wife Package. She makes them because Nona taught her that when people come into your house, you feed them. She bakes because she grew up doing it with Nona. Entertaining isn’t a performance of femininity; it’s family culture that Chloe genuinely loves.

That also makes the friend scene much better. They can still mercilessly dog-pile Allison—because from the outside the optics are hysterical—but Chloe can puncture the mythology herself:

“Guys, I’m not perfect. My nona just considered letting someone sit in your house without feeding them a character defect.”

Now Chloe can tell Nona stories, including funny ones. Maybe Nona had very definite rules about hospitality, cooking, cleaning, or what constituted an acceptable amount of food. Chloe learned skills from an actual person, with memories attached, rather than mysteriously possessing every domestic competency the plot requires.

And we shouldn’t make every dish spectacular either. Chloe can have things she’s excellent at, things she makes because Nona made them, and things she routinely screws up. That’s warmer and funnier.

It also gives Steve and Dot’s family culture another generation behind it. Chloe’s practical caretaking didn’t materialize with her transition; this is how the Johnson family loves people, with Nona as one of the people who taught Chloe the language.

One thing I would not invent yet is which side of the family Nona belongs to, whether she’s living, her ethnicity/background, or Chloe’s exact childhood history with her. Those are consequential biography. But Nona as the source of Chloe’s love of feeding and entertaining people feels like the piece you’re establishing right now.
