# Age & Generational Culture

Status: ACTIVE CULTURE AND CRAFT REFERENCE
Authority: REUSABLE GUIDANCE; INDIVIDUAL CHARACTER VARIATION CONTROLS


A story-agnostic reference toolkit for writing characters whose age or generation shapes how they talk, what they reference, and how they relate to institutions and technology. A character's generation and current life stage are different axes and should not be collapsed into one another.

## Generational Cohorts (rough, contested boundaries — treat as guidelines, not hard cutoffs)

- **Silent Generation** (~1928–1945): came of age around WWII/postwar recovery; formative frugality from the Depression era in their own parents/childhood; strong deference to institutions and formal authority; radio and early TV as childhood media; often reached retirement age before the internet was mainstream.
- **Baby Boomers** (~1946–1964): postwar prosperity, the Civil Rights era, Vietnam and the draft, moon landing, the rise of TV as a shared cultural experience; entered the workforce expecting long-term single-employer careers and pensions; adopted computers/internet as adults mid-career (a genuine, specific learning-curve moment, not permanent illiteracy); landline-and-answering-machine phone culture; often skeptical of employer loyalty being reciprocated after witnessing layoffs later in their careers.
- **Gen X** (~1965–1980): latchkey-kid upbringing (both parents working became common), MTV, the AIDS crisis backdrop, the end of the Cold War, arcade and early home-console gaming, cassette tapes/mixtapes, learned computers as teens/young adults (the actual "digital bridge" generation — comfortable with tech but not born into it), known for a self-described skeptical/independent streak and dislike of corporate rah-rah culture.
- **Millennials** (~1981–1996): grew up alongside the internet's mainstreaming (dial-up to broadband), 9/11 as a formative shared memory, the 2008 financial crisis hitting right as many entered the job market (a real, specific economic-anxiety touchstone), participation trophies/helicopter parenting discourse (often resented as a flattening stereotype by Millennials themselves), social media adoption in their teens/20s (AIM, MySpace, then Facebook), student debt as a defining financial fact for many.
- **Gen Z** (~1997–2012): smartphones and social media (Instagram, Snapchat, TikTok) present from adolescence or earlier, school-shooting-drill childhood as a genuine formative backdrop in the US, climate anxiety as an explicit generational touchstone, COVID-19 lockdowns hitting during formative school/early-career years, comfortable moving fluidly between texting/DMs/video as default communication rather than phone calls (many actively dislike unscheduled phone calls), grew up with a searchable internet and AI tools arriving during their teens/20s.
- **Gen Alpha** (~2013–present): tablets and touchscreens from early childhood, YouTube/streaming rather than broadcast TV as the default video experience, growing up with generative AI tools already normalized rather than novel, still mostly children/teens as of the mid-2020s — write with care and less certainty since this cohort's adult culture hasn't fully formed yet.

## Technology Relationship & Adoption Timeline

Each generation has a genuine "first exposure" moment for major tech rather than uniform lifelong familiarity: rotary/corded phones → cordless → flip phones → smartphones; broadcast TV → cable → streaming; dial-up → broadband → mobile-always-on internet. The specific age at which a character encountered a technology (as a child taking it for granted vs. as an adult consciously learning it) is the real texture — an older character being slow with a new app isn't blanket incompetence, it's a specific, learnable unfamiliarity, while a younger character isn't reflexively expert at everything digital (a teenager can be bad at formal email or phone calls precisely because they skipped that step).

## Communication Style & Norms

Phone calls vs. texting vs. DMs vs. email carry different default formality and urgency by generation — an unscheduled phone call reads as normal to older generations and as alarming/rude to many younger ones. Emoji/punctuation conventions differ (a period at the end of a text reading as curt or passive-aggressive to younger texters is a real, specific, useful detail). Workplace communication norms differ (formal memos and scheduled meetings vs. Slack-style async messaging) and are a genuine source of friction rather than a joke with no substance behind it.

## Attitudes Toward Work & Institutions

Expectations of employer loyalty, career-length-at-one-company, pensions vs. 401(k)-style self-directed retirement, and trust in institutions (government, media, employers) vary meaningfully by cohort and by what each generation watched happen to the generation before them — useful as backstory logic for a character's specific attitude rather than a generic "generation X is entitled/lazy" flatness.

## Cross-Generational Dynamics

Family scenes: differing views on privacy, money discussion norms (older generations often treat salary/money as taboo to discuss; younger cohorts more often discuss it openly, partly as a labor-organizing/pay-transparency norm), differing default communication channel causing real missed-connection friction (a grandparent expecting a phone call, a grandchild only checking texts). Workplace scenes: a multi-generational team genuinely differs in meeting style, feedback expectations, and tool comfort — write this as two people solving the same problem with different defaults, not one side simply being right.

## Common Outsider Tells

Writing an older character as uniformly tech-illiterate rather than unfamiliar-with-specific-things-they-haven't-encountered-yet; giving a younger character every current slang term indiscriminately regardless of their specific subculture/region (slang varies hugely within a generation, not just between them); treating generational labels ("boomer," "zoomer") as precise scientific categories rather than the loose, contested, often-jokingly-used cohort terms they actually are; using a single dated slang word as the entire characterization of a generation; and forgetting that individual variation within a generation is often larger than the average difference between generations — the cohort is a useful default, not a determinism.

## Workflow

1. A character's generation needs to read as specific: place their birth year in a cohort above and pull the concrete formative touchstone, tech-adoption point, and communication default that fits — rather than a generic "old" or "young" flatness.
2. A scene needs generational friction (family or workplace): frame it as two genuinely different defaults colliding, not one character simply being right and the other simply being behind.
3. When a scene needs both cohort and a current life-stage event, establish each separately—for example, a Gen X character's experience of menopause rather than an abstract generic menopause scene.
4. Slang/reference lists date fast — favor the structural patterns above (formality defaults, tech-adoption timing, formative events) over chasing the newest slang, which will read as dated within a year or two.
