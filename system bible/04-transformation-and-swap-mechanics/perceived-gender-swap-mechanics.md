*A new sub-category under Module 1, Section 0. Distinct from both Swap and Change as originally defined, because the aspect that moves doesn't belong to either character's own body, mind, or trait — it's the incoming perception of every outside observer. Proposed as a new Section 3E, parallel to 3A (Transfer Package) and 3B (Linked Aspects), for any future instance where a swap or change targets perception rather than the subject.*

## Core Distinction (extends Section 0)

- **Physical/Mental Swap or Change (existing definitions):** the aspect itself — body, mind, trait — actually moves or alters for the subject.
- **Perceptual Swap or Change (new):** nothing about the subject's actual body, mind, or trait moves. What moves is every outside observer's perception of the subject. The subject is the same in every respect that matters to physics; only how they are seen, heard, and treated changes.

## Section 3E: Perceptual Layer Package

*Fill this in for any instance where a swap or change targets perception. Companion to 3A/3B, not a replacement — use 3E instead of 3A/3B when the transferred aspect is observer perception rather than something the subject possesses.*

- **Mechanism Class:** Swap (mutual exchange between two specific individuals) or Change (one party's perceived attribute shifts, no counterpart). *This instance: Swap, between Tom and Nora.*
- **Perceptual Bundle Scope:** the full gender-read of the person — body/silhouette, facial features, voice, and all downstream social treatment that follows from that read (pronouns, titles/forms of address, default assumptions about role — customer vs. companion, bathroom/facility assumptions, institutional treatment). Per the toolkit's existing Default Depth principle, naming the aspect with no restricting qualifier means the full bundle applies; "only" is what narrows it.
- **Explicitly Not a Separate Target:** clothing, objects, and possessions are never part of the perceptual bundle and don't need a carve-out — they're simply seen accurately, as they are, because they were never targeted. Any incongruity (a dress on a perceived-male body, a hair appointment "wasted" on the wrong-perceived person) is a *consequence* that falls naturally out of the swap, not a rule that has to be separately written.
- **Uniformity:** every observer perceives the same swapped read, identically. No variance between observers, no partial seeing-through.
- **Continuity Model:** real-time discontinuity. Observers do not retroactively remember the swapped state as having always been true — the moment of the trigger is a live event, even though its aftermath (assumptions, treatment, service) proceeds as though nothing is unusual. **Steady-state note:** this discontinuity is a one-time event, not an ongoing tension. Once an observer has recalibrated, day-to-day behavior becomes functionally indistinguishable from "this has always been true" — not because the memory of the change is erased, but because nothing in the Illusion Confidence Rule gives anyone a reason to revisit it. Avoid writing observers who nostalgically reference the change itself ("remember when you got that done") days or weeks out — that reintroduces doubt the rule doesn't allow. The trigger moment stays a real, rememberable event if a scene ever specifically needs to surface it; it simply never surfaces on its own.
- **Ground-Truth Exemption:** both parties to the swap retain full, accurate perception of themselves *and of each other*, mutually, for the duration. They are the only two people for whom nothing has changed.
- **Correction Resistance (the Illusion Confidence Rule):** this is the load-bearing craft principle and applies to every instance, not just this one. Observers under the illusion experience zero internal doubt, hesitation, or dissonance — their perception is not a mistake they're one clue away from catching, it's simply what's true for them, held with the same total confidence anyone holds any obviously true thing. Practical consequences for scene-writing:
  - Do **not** write lingering bystander doubt, repeated double-takes, or visible unresolved confusion in observers — a flinch or hitch is fine as a brief, real-time reaction to the swap's *arrival*, but it resolves fast and doesn't recur once an observer has recalibrated.
  - A correction offered by the swapped party is not *refuted* by observers — there's nothing to refute. It's absorbed, reinterpreted, or overtaken by momentum (a half-finished sentence, a joke taken as confirmation, a professional already in motion) rather than argued against.
  - Service, care, or feedback given under the illusion is rendered at the observer's normal competence — the outcome can be genuinely good. The horror is not incompetence; it's that a well-executed, kindly-meant result can be fundamentally non-consensual and wrong despite being visibly excellent, which is harder to point at and contest than a bad outcome would be.
  - The swapped party's own emotional arc under this rule tends toward disorientation and eventual withdrawal (giving up on correcting it in the moment) rather than escalating conflict, since there is no opponent in the scene to escalate against — only a room full of people being ordinary and kind according to what they perceive.
  - **Voluntold variant:** plans, invitations, and logistics are frequently phrased so there's no real request to respond to in the first place — "Saturday's still good, right?", "I already added you to the chat" — rather than an open question a swapped character could hesitate over or decline. This forecloses the moment of correction before it can start, which reads as more unsettling than a hesitation that gets absorbed after the fact. Prefer this phrasing over an offered invitation whenever the scene calls for something already treated as settled by the people arranging it.

- **Conformity Pressure Arc:** a separate principle from the Illusion Confidence Rule above, describing how pressure escalates *over time and repetition* rather than sitting still after the initial mismatch. Certainty about the underlying premise (the Illusion Confidence Rule) never wavers, but that certainty combines with a baseline expectation of what the perceived gender *should* look like — and a swapped character who doesn't meet it reads to observers not as evidence anything is wrong with their own perception, but as evidence something is wrong with the character, worth caring about and fixing. The arc tends to move through recognizable stages across a scene or story: (1) mismatched assumptions and treatment, largely unremarked; (2) a noticed *absence* or pattern — opting out of a gendered ritual, a look that hasn't kept up — that observers name directly rather than let slide; (3) organized, well-meant intervention aimed at closing the gap (makeup, wardrobe, coaching), offered as care rather than correction. This is never driven by hostility — the same warmth and confidence from the Illusion Confidence Rule carries through every stage — but the cumulative effect is genuine pressure on the swapped character to either begin conforming to the perceived gender's presentation or continue absorbing rising friction with no ally in the room who shares their view of what's actually happening.

- **Gendered Asymmetry in Consequence — story-selectable, not a project-wide default:** the Tom/Nora cold test explored a nurturing response to perceived failure of feminine presentation and a punitive response to perceived failure of masculine presentation. That contrast may be selected for a particular story, culture, workplace, or cast, but it is not a universal social law and cannot be inferred merely from gender. Establish the relevant people and institution before using it.
