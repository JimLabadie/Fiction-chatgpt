# Sapphic Symbols & Visual Culture

## Symbols & Visual Culture

A character's jewelry, tattoo, or a decorated object can carry real, specific meaning — useful for subtle recognition-between-strangers scenes as well as visible pride. Weight varies hugely by symbol: some are cheerful in-jokes, others carry real historical trauma, so match tone to register rather than flattening everything into decoration.

- **Violets:** trace to Sappho (~600 BCE, the island of Lesbos), who used violets in her poetry as a symbol of desire between women. Revived in 1926 when Édouard Bourdet's play *The Captive* (a scandalous same-sex love story) featured a bouquet of violets as a love token onstage; queer audience members began wearing violets afterward as a quiet show of solidarity — a lapel violet or one tucked into a hat as a subtle signal.
- **Scissors:** references "scissoring"/tribadism, a term with roots in 19th-century writing about sexual contact between women (ultimately from Greek *tribein*, "to rub"). Functions today mostly as an outsider stereotype/punchline about lesbian sex rather than in-community shorthand — some have reclaimed it as a knowing inside joke about how outsiders imagine their sex lives, but playing it straight as if it's how the community actually talks reads as an outsider tell (see below).
- **Snake:** a 2020s/Gen-Z-associated sapphic symbol (tattoos, jewelry, rings), drawing on snakes' associations with defiance and being demonized (the Eden narrative), with transformation and shedding skin as a metaphor for coming out or embracing a fluid identity, and older mythic ties to renewal and rebirth (the Greek ouroboros, Mayan/Aztec rebirth symbolism, the Indian serpent goddess Nagini, African wisdom-serpent traditions).
- **Interlocking double Venus (♀♀):** two linked "female" symbols as shorthand for love between women; the Venus symbol itself references the Roman goddess of love and beauty. The doubled version became common on pins, jewelry, and flags by the 20th century, closely tied to feminism as well as lesbian identity specifically.
- **Black triangle:** in Nazi concentration camps, women were marked with a black triangle under the broad, dehumanizing "asocial" classification (which also swept in sex workers and disabled people) rather than being acknowledged as queer at all — a double erasure, denying identity while still marking someone for persecution. 1970s lesbian feminists reclaimed it as a badge of resilience, parallel to gay men reclaiming the pink triangle. Treat as real historical weight, not casual decoration.
- **Lavender (color & word):** the 1950s U.S. "Lavender Scare" purged thousands of suspected gay men and women from federal jobs as alleged security risks. In 1969 Betty Friedan called politically visible lesbians within the feminist movement a "lavender menace," fearing they'd discredit the cause for women's equality; in 1970 a group of lesbian feminists reclaimed the label outright, wearing hand-dyed lavender "Lavender Menace" shirts as they took the stage at the Second Congress to Unite Women — turning an insult into an organizing identity and forcing a conversation about inclusion within feminism itself.
- **Labrys (double-headed axe):** rooted in Minoan Crete goddess worship and Amazon warrior lore, tied to matriarchal strength and autonomy. Reclaimed by 1970s lesbian feminists as a symbol of resistance. Appeared on the 1999 labrys flag (a white labrys over an inverted black triangle on a violet field) — among the oldest explicitly lesbian flags, though it never gained wide adoption, partly because the black triangle's trauma history felt too heavy for some to fly casually.
- **Nautical star:** originated among 1950s lesbians in Buffalo, NY, worn specifically as a wrist tattoo because it could be hidden under a watch — a practical, discreet way to signal belonging to an underground community when visibility risked real harassment or job loss. Still used today in tattoos and jewelry as a nod to that lineage.
- **Sunset pride flag:** the widely-adopted "sunset" flag (pinks, oranges, purples, white) superseded the 2010 "lipstick lesbian" flag (pink tones with a lipstick-kiss mark in the corner), which drew criticism for centering only feminine-presenting lesbians and excluding others. The newer gradient, popularized around 2018, reads as more inclusive. Individual stripes carry commonly cited meanings (dark orange = gender nonconformity, orange = independence, light orange = community, white = diverse relationships to womanhood, pink = serenity/peace, dusty pink = love and sex, purple/rose = femininity) — useful for a character who specifically cares about flag history, though most people flying it wouldn't recite the stripe meanings from memory.
- **Carabiner clipped to a belt loop:** traces to WWII-era women entering industrial and blue-collar work, who used carabiners functionally; by the 1970s, feminists (lesbians especially) adopted them as a practical, unfussy accessory that rejected delicate/decorative femininity. Had a visible resurgence in the 2020s, especially among masc-presenting and chapstick-lesbian style — also a practical response to women's clothing chronically lacking real pockets.
- **Vulva-adjacent imagery** (peaches, oysters, flowers): draws most famously on Georgia O'Keeffe's flower paintings as the best-known art-historical link between floral imagery and the female form. Functions as a reclamation of a body that's historically been shamed or objectified, echoing 1960s–70s feminist body-reclamation politics rather than being explicit content itself.
- **Monocle:** worn by some lesbians in the 1920s–30s as a discreet signal, borrowing a fashion piece previously associated with aristocratic men. Tied to real spaces — Le Monocle, an early lesbian club in Paris that ran into the 1940s until the Nazi occupation closed it, and Damenklub Violetta in Berlin, which threw a 1928 "Monokelfest" event where attendees received a free monocle.
- **Sappho, Lesbos, Pleiades, and constellations:** literary and jewelry references only where the specific design or character establishes them.
