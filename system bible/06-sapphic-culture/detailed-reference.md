## Vocabulary & Self-Description

Core identity terms, by generation: lesbian, sapphic, wlw (women-loving-women), gay (used by women too, not just men), queer as reclaimed umbrella term (contested reclamation — see below). Bisexual women are a distinct experience, not interchangeable with lesbian identity — don't collapse them. "Comphet" (compulsory heterosexuality) is a newer, mostly-online-era term for the pressure to perform straightness; "lesbian until graduation" is an old dismissive phrase, sometimes now used ironically by the community itself. Generational friction is real and usable: many older lesbians prefer "lesbian" or "gay" over "queer" (a slur they lived through, not one they grew up reclaiming), while younger cohorts often default to "queer" as the umbrella term.

**Style/presentation labels:** butch (masculine presentation — short hair, popped collars, "gentlemanly" manners, per common community shorthand), stone butch (specifically doesn't like to be touched sexually, prefers giving to receiving — distinct from butch presentation alone), bulldagger/bulldyke/dieseldyke (progressively more intense masculine-presentation terms, the last associated with trucks/heavy machinery/plaid), femme (feminine presentation — nails, makeup, "girly" clothes, per common shorthand), lipstick lesbian (very femme), chapstick lesbian / stem (in-between, neither fully butch nor femme — "stud/femme" blend), androgynous/andro/boi (presenting as neither firmly butch nor femme, or boyish; "boi" specifically carries several overlapping meanings depending on community), soft butch (butch-presenting but without the harder edges of stone butch), sporty dyke (athletic presentation/interests), drag king (a woman performing as a man, usually for performance specifically — distinct from butch as an everyday identity). Stud and aggressive/AG are terms rooted specifically in Black lesbian community usage for a dominant, typically butch-presenting lesbian.

**Relationship/dating terms:** "U-Haul" for moving in together fast (see Symbols below for the joke's origin); "friendcest" for the small-scene pattern of having slept with or dated several people in the same friend circle (the actual community term for what's described below under Relationship Scripts); "partner" as a word that often supersedes "girlfriend" once a relationship is established/serious; "beard" for an opposite-sex partner used to disguise being closeted; "marble" (MARried but LEsbian) for a woman who stays in a heterosexual marriage while privately identifying as gay.

**Terms that carry real in-community judgment — useful for tension/conflict, not neutral labels:** gold star (never having slept with a man — a real but contested, often-criticized hierarchy/purity framing); hasbian (someone who returns to opposite-sex relationships after a period identifying as lesbian — often used dismissively); saysbian (dismissive term implying someone claims the identity without "really" living it); baby dyke (young/new to the community, sometimes said with mild condescension); Saturday-night lesbian, beersexual, bicurious, trysexual (various framings, often skeptical ones, for situational or exploratory same-sex attraction) — a character using these about someone else signals real judgment, not just description.

**Community/culture terms:** dyke (a reclaimed slur — the Dyke March exists specifically around reclaiming it, distinct from mainstream Pride), gayborhood (a neighborhood/venue with a visible queer concentration), gaydar, closet/coming out/outed, pride, LGBTQQIA+ as an inclusive-umbrella shorthand, "the lipstick mafia" and "the muffia" as affectionate, half-joking terms for tight-knit or well-connected lesbian social/professional networks (the latter evoking a Bette-from-The-L-Word archetype: successful, moneyed, intimidating). Sex-role vocabulary (top, bottom, pillow princess, vanilla) functions as ordinary identity/community shorthand, not explicit content — useful the way any relationship-dynamics vocabulary is. Note "packing" has two distinct senses depending on context: a strap-on worn under clothes for presentation/role-play (the sense in this dictionary) versus a trans/nonbinary character's gender-affirming packer, covered in `gender-presentation-culture` — don't conflate the two uses in one scene without the story making clear which is meant.

**Terms to use knowingly, as outsider framing rather than sincere self-description:** "golden penis" (mocking men who believe they can "cure" a lesbian), "malesbian" (a straight man who identifies as a lesbian — typically viewed with real skepticism/humor in-community), "scissoring" (see Symbols below — largely how outsiders imagine lesbian sex, not in-community shorthand), "vagitarian" (a joking euphemism).

## Historical Touchstones

Stonewall (1969) was significantly driven by trans women of color (Sylvia Rivera, Marsha P. Johnson) — be careful not to flatten this into a lesbian-specific or gay-male-only story. Lesbian feminist separatism (1970s) and the lesbian "sex wars" (1980s, pro-sex vs. anti-porn feminist factions) are real internal-community fault lines worth knowing before writing an older character's politics. Lesbians were heavily and disproportionately involved in caregiving during the AIDS crisis despite facing low personal medical risk — a real, moving, under-dramatized piece of history. The Lesbian Avengers (1992, direct-action group) and The L Word (2004, huge cultural touchstone with a genuinely mixed community reception, and the source of the "the Muffia"/Bette archetype above) are useful reference points for a character with historical memory or opinions. The 1950s Lavender Scare and the Buffalo nautical-star tradition (both above, under Symbols) are useful grounding for any story reaching back to mid-century lesbian life specifically, when visibility carried real professional and legal risk.

# Sapphic Culture

Authority: SHARED-WORLD CANON FOR MEANING NOT UNIVERSAL BEHAVIOR

Sapphic culture is plural, generational, regional, racial, and personal. Symbols and phrases may signal recognition or community, but none proves identity. A character may know, miss, misunderstand, reclaim, or deliberately use them.

## Language

- **Sapphic:** an inclusive cultural and relational term centered on women loving women; usage varies.
- **Lesbian:** an identity, community, and history, not merely an act or anatomy.
- **Egg:** informal trans-community language for someone who has not recognized or acknowledged being trans. It is a metaphor, not a diagnosis others own.
- **Butch, femme, masc:** identities or presentations with community histories; they do not assign compulsory relationship power.
- **Chosen family:** durable kinship built through care and commitment.

Archive slang lists are reference pools, not dialogue menus. Match terms to character, generation, place, knowledge, and voice.

The [Detailed Sapphic Culture Reference](06-sapphic-culture/detailed-reference.md) preserves the developed vocabulary, community spaces, relationship lore, symbols and visual history, presentation spectrum, historical touchstones, and outsider-error guidance. Use it for specific texture while preserving character choice and generational, regional, racial, and personal variation.

## Social practice

Community care includes respecting self-identification and not publicly outing someone. A stranger may notice something without owning the right to announce it. Attraction and safety judgments depend on behavior and context, not anatomy alone. A trans woman is not an exception to womanhood, and sapphic relationships do not become heterosexual because of anatomy.
