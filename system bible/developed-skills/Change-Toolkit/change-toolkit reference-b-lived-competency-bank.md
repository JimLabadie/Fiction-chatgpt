# Change Toolkit — Reference B: Lived Competency Bank

*A shared bank of codified, typical/normal-life knowledge — routines, habits, terminology, social patterns, and life-course events — organized by identity axis (age, gender, occupation, subculture, etc.).*

> **Instructions for AI Context Integration:** This is a reference, not a fillable log — read it whenever a scene involves a character living as, performing, or being coached into an identity or role that isn't their own (per a Change-dynamic instance in Module 1), whenever a Perceptual Swap/Change (Module 1 Section 3E) requires a character to convincingly pass, or whenever a story introduces a life event/milestone a character needs to navigate. Pull concrete factual content from here rather than inventing ad hoc "typical" details on the fly, so the same baseline reads the same way every time it comes up across a story — or across different stories using this toolkit. How much of a given entry any specific character already knows, needs to learn, or has already lived through is a per-story decision made in that story's own instance file — this bank only supplies the underlying facts, not who has them. Per Module 1's existing guardrail, a donor's or coach's teaching should reflect specific, lived judgment (drawn from a named character's actual habits per Section 3C) rather than a generic recitation of any list below — treat these entries as the underlying vocabulary of *what* might need learning or navigating, not a script to be read verbatim in dialogue.

## Axis: Age — Teen Girl (Approx. 15–19)

### Morning Routine — Skincare & Body Care

*Sequence note: this tier of routine is typically completed in full before getting dressed, not worked into a dressing routine piecemeal.*

- **Exfoliation:** Full-body exfoliation, often including legs and arms, using a scrub (shea-butter based or similar) — several times a week rather than daily for most.
- **Leg/body hair removal:** Shaving legs (and underarms) is commonly folded into the same shower/routine block as exfoliation, typically a few times a week rather than daily.
- **Facial serum:** A targeted serum — vitamin C is a common choice for brightening/evening tone — applied to clean, dry skin.
- **Hydration layer:** A hyaluronic-acid-based hydrating product layered after serum, before heavier moisturizer.
- **Moisturizer/lotion:** Scented body lotion applied head to toe as a finishing step; a separate, often unscented or lighter facial moisturizer for the face specifically.
- **Sunscreen:** Commonly the last skincare step before makeup, even on cloudy days, for anyone routine-conscious.

### Morning Routine — Hair

- Detangling and a quick style (braid, ponytail, half-up) is far more common on a school/weekday morning than a full blowout or heat styling, which tends to be reserved for going-out days.
- Dry shampoo use between wash days is common.
- Heat protectant spray before any flat iron/curling iron use, when heat styling is done.

### Morning Routine — Makeup (If Worn)

- Weekday/school makeup, when worn at all, tends toward minimal: concealer or light coverage, brow gel, mascara, tinted lip balm or gloss.
- Skincare-adjacent "no-makeup makeup" trends (tinted moisturizer, cream blush) are common in this age group specifically.
- A separate, more involved "going out" makeup routine (foundation, eyeliner, more elaborate) typically exists alongside the everyday one — the two are not the same routine.

### Morning Routine — Timing & Structure

- Full routine (skincare + hair + makeup + dressing) commonly runs anywhere from 20–45 minutes on a school day, longer on weekends or event days.
- Getting dressed is typically the last step, after skincare/hair/makeup are complete — clothing choice is rarely decided first.
- Checking phone (messages, social media) is commonly interspersed throughout the routine rather than done as a separate, discrete step.

### Common Outsider Tells (What an Adult Newcomer to This Routine Would Get Wrong)

- Skipping the skincare-before-dressing sequence — getting dressed first, then trying to do skincare/makeup after, which reads as backwards to anyone used to the routine.
- Underestimating how many product "layers" go on the face before makeup (serum → hydration → moisturizer → sunscreen → makeup), rather than treating it as one or two steps.
- Not knowing the distinction between a weekday routine and a going-out routine — treating every day as requiring the same effort level, or the reverse.
- Misjudging routine length — either assuming it's much faster (5 minutes) or much more elaborate than it typically is for an ordinary day.

## Axis: Shower & Body-Care Routine (General — Not Age-Restricted)

*Unlike the Teen Girl axis above, this axis is deliberately age-general — use for any character whose established habits include a routine-conscious feminine shower/body-care regimen, regardless of age. Specific product names are illustrative, not prescriptive — real, currently-popular examples included to show the category and level of specificity a routine-conscious character would operate at. Products and trends date quickly; treat them as swappable placeholders, or substitute the story's own established brand preferences per Module 1 Section 3C.*

### Cleansing

- A dedicated body wash, often scent-driven — routine-conscious characters frequently have a signature scent they're recognized by, not just a functional cleanser. (Illustrative examples: a Brazilian-inspired gourmand scent like pistachio/salted caramel; a soft floral blend like peony, rose, and sea salt.)
- Scent choice often functions as an identity marker in-story — someone recognizing a character "by smell" before seeing them is a realistic, usable beat.

### Exfoliation (Pre-Shave)

- A body scrub (commonly shea- or sugar-based) used before shaving specifically to prevent ingrown hairs and razor bumps — this is a distinct step from general cleansing, not a substitute for it.
- An exfoliating mitt or tool as an alternative/addition to a scrub, used to prep skin for a closer shave.
- This step is skipped more often when time-pressured (rushing before an event) — a realistic detail for scenes needing time pressure.

### Shaving

- A multi-blade razor (5-blade systems are common in current routines) rather than a basic disposable, often marketed/packaged specifically for body use.
- A dedicated, non-foaming shave cream or gel (as distinct from bar soap) — valued for moisture retention and reducing razor burn, and often scented to coordinate with or complement the body wash.
- Common shave zones for a routine-conscious character: legs (full, including knees and ankles — commonly cited trouble spots for nicks), underarms, and bikini line, in that general order within a shower.

### Post-Shave Moisture & Finishing

- A fast-absorbing body cream applied immediately post-shower while skin is still slightly damp, often from the same scent line as the body wash for a coordinated effect.
- A supplementary hydrating lotion (often with actives like hyaluronic acid or an AHA) layered on for texture/smoothness, distinct from the "finishing scent" cream above — routine-conscious characters often use two distinct products here, not one.

### Hair Care (In-Shower)

- A detox or clarifying shampoo used specifically to strip product buildup — sweat, scalp oil, hairspray, dry shampoo residue — heavier-duty than a daily shampoo, and typically not used every wash.
- A deep-conditioning mask used periodically (not every wash) to counteract damage from heat styling or tight hairstyles/ties.
- For characters with an athletic or performance-heavy lifestyle (cheer, dance, sports), hair care in this routine is often explicitly framed around undoing the day's physical stress on hair and scalp, not just cleaning.

### Sequencing Note

A full routine-conscious shower typically runs in this order: cleanse → exfoliate → shave → rinse → in-shower hair treatment (if applicable) → exit and apply post-shave moisture while skin is still damp. Skipping the damp-skin timing on the moisture step is a realistic small error for a character new to the routine (see Common Outsider Tells) — lotion applied to fully dry skin behaves and feels different, and a coach/support character might specifically correct this.

*This axis pairs with, but is distinct from, the Teen Girl Morning Routine section (full pre-dressing routine including makeup/styling) and the Gender Socialization axis below (behavioral/social logic of grooming labor generally). This section is the concrete step-by-step shower/shave content itself, reusable at any age a story needs it.*

## Axis: Gender Socialization — Learned Behavioral Patterns

*Applicable across ages, not specific to teens — use whenever a character is learning to pass as, or being coached into presenting as, a gender other than the one they were socialized into. Distinct from the physical/presentation rules already governed by Module 1 Section 3B and the Presentation Matrix; this axis covers behavioral/social conditioning.*

### Non-Verbal Communication & Body Language

- **Spatial claiming:** Sitting with legs apart / a wider seated footprint, versus knees and ankles held close together in a compact, closed posture.
- **Walking dynamics:** Movement driven from the shoulders and upper torso with a wider stride, versus movement driven from the hips with a narrower, more fluid foot placement.
- **Arm and elbow placement:** Elbows flared outward (claiming adjacent space, e.g., on shared armrests), versus elbows tucked in against the torso.
- **Resting facial expression:** A neutral or stoic default expression, versus a habitual soft smile or open, "approachable" resting face — including unconscious self-monitoring for whether the resting face is being read as unfriendly.
- **Gaze and eye contact:** Sustained, direct, sometimes challenging eye contact, versus more frequent glancing away, softer or shorter eye contact used to de-escalate or signal deference.
- **Hand gestures:** Larger, more expansive gestures that use surrounding space, versus smaller, closer-to-the-body gestures.
- **Yielding personal space:** Holding one's ground/line/position in crowded public space, versus an automatic, often unconscious habit of stepping aside or giving way first.
- **Posture and spine alignment:** Chest lifted, shoulders broadened/squared, versus a more gathered, aligned posture often read as "graceful" or "poised."
- **Head tilting:** A learned habit of tilting the head slightly during conversation to visibly signal active listening/empathy — often absent or unconscious in the other socialization pattern.
- **Fidgeting/self-soothing under stress:** Rhythmic tapping or pacing, versus smaller self-soothing touches — adjusting hair, jewelry, or clothing.

### Verbal Communication & Conversational Dynamics

- **Interruption norms:** Differing social permission to interrupt in group conversation, and differing reactions to being interrupted (calling it out directly vs. absorbing it and waiting).
- **Pitch and vocal modulation:** Deliberately lowering/flattening pitch to register as authoritative, versus a wider, more variable pitch range used to convey warmth or enthusiasm.
- **Hedging language:** Habitual softeners ("I might be wrong, but—," "this is probably a dumb question, but—") before a request or opinion, versus stating the same thing as a flat, unhedged declaration.
- **Active back-channeling:** Frequent verbal agreement cues during someone else's turn to talk ("right," "totally," "oh my god") to sustain the conversational flow, versus staying comparatively quiet until it's one's own turn to speak.
- **Apologetic reflexes:** Prefacing ordinary requests or minor mistakes with an unprompted "sorry" — often reflexive and not tied to genuine fault — a habit that reads as conspicuously present or conspicuously absent depending on socialization.
- **Storytelling structure:** Leading with the bottom line/conclusion first, versus narrating a fuller arc with context and emotional detail before the point.
- **Compliment patterns:** Complimenting competence/intellect as a default, versus complimenting appearance, style, or warmth as a default — including discomfort or surprise at receiving the "wrong" kind.
- **Public-speaking presence:** Differing comfort levels with projecting to fill a room, taking up rhetorical space, and holding the floor in mixed-group settings.
- **Conflict style:** Direct, face-to-face confrontation of a disagreement, versus indirect approaches aimed at consensus or letting tension resolve without a direct clash.
- **Humor style:** Fast, competitive one-upmanship in group banter, versus more cooperative, inclusive joke-building that invites others in rather than "winning" the exchange.

### Social Etiquette & Public Safety

- **Environmental scanning:** Habitual safety checks — glancing at a car's backseat before getting in, holding keys between fingers while walking to a car at night, automatically noting exits in an unfamiliar room — that are second nature to one socialization and rarely occur to mind in the other.
- **Nighttime mobility:** Altering a walking route after dark, choosing footwear that allows running, texting a friend an ETA or "arrived safe" check-in — habits that may be entirely absent from someone's prior life and need active, conscious adoption.
- **Street interactions with strangers:** Calculating whether a polite nod/acknowledgment is safe versus deliberately avoiding eye contact or engagement with an unfamiliar person approaching on the street.
- **Gatekeeping/courtesy norms:** Unspoken rules about who holds the door, who's expected to offer a seat, elevator entry order, who initiates a greeting first.
- **Networking venues and norms:** Where professional rapport-building is expected to happen (golf course, mixer, informal after-hours settings) and the unspoken rules of who gets included.
- **Emotional-labor default:** An unspoken expectation to be the one remembering birthdays, organizing group gatherings, or smoothing over family friction — versus not being expected to track any of it.
- **Asking for help publicly:** Overcoming an internalized resistance to asking a stranger for assistance, versus feeling social pressure to visibly solve a problem alone rather than ask.
- **Credit and self-effacement:** Comfort claiming individual credit for a group win out loud, versus a learned instinct to downplay one's own role even when it was central.
- **Hosting reflex:** An automatic pull toward the kitchen to help prep/clean at a gathering, versus a default expectation to remain seated as a guest.
- **Gift-giving expectations:** Different unspoken thresholds for what counts as a thoughtful vs. cheap or excessive gift at a given milestone.

### Appearance, Maintenance & Grooming Rituals

*Cross-reference: the Teen Girl Morning Routine and Shower & Body-Care Routine sections above cover concrete routine detail; this section covers the broader behavioral logic behind grooming labor, applicable past the teen years.*

- **Daily maintenance load:** The sheer time/step-count difference in a "normal" day's skincare, hair styling, and hair-removal routine — this is a structural time-budget difference, not just a preference difference.
- **Wardrobe mechanics:** Managing clothing with more fasteners, adjustable-but-fiddly closures, shapewear, or footwear that alters posture and gait (heels) — all of which take practiced skill to move naturally in, not instinct.
- **Accessorizing as an active skill:** Coordinating jewelry, bag, belt, and scarf choices as a considered daily decision rather than an afterthought.
- **Cosmetic application:** A genuine technical skill set — color-matching, blending, symmetry between the two sides of a face, knowing when product needs reapplying — not something performed correctly on a first attempt without practice.
- **Public restroom conduct:** Different unspoken codes — conversational norms at a shared mirror/sink area versus strict silence and no eye contact in a stall-based space.
- **Sizing literacy:** Learning that clothing sizing is brand-inconsistent and requires trying-on rather than trusting a labeled size, especially in a wardrobe category the character has no prior experience shopping for.
- **Carrying daily items:** Adjusting from pockets-only habits to managing a bag — what goes in it, keeping track of it, treating it as an extension of "being dressed."
- **Weather trade-offs:** Choosing a cold- or heat-uncomfortable outfit because of a presentation goal, and having a tolerance built from practice rather than the first time it's attempted.
- **Mirror-checking habit:** A frequent, often unconscious habit of checking reflection/appearance in passing mirrors, windows, or a phone camera — versus rarely doing this at all.
- **Scent management:** Deliberately selecting and applying fragrance for a given occasion or environment as a considered step, not an afterthought.

### Psychological, Emotional & Relational Navigation

- **Emotionally acceptable outlets:** Which emotions are socially permitted to show openly (anger vs. sadness, for instance) and which get suppressed or redirected — a real internal habit, not just an external performance.
- **Friendship-bonding style:** Bonding primarily through doing an activity side-by-side, versus bonding through direct, face-to-face emotional conversation.
- **Risk tolerance signaling:** Differing social permission/expectation around visible physical risk-taking or thrill-seeking.
- **Mentorship access:** Navigating who tends to informally sponsor or advocate for someone in a professional setting, and the unwritten rules of how that relationship is built.
- **Romantic-pursuit scripts:** Who's expected to initiate, who's expected to pay, and the expected way to handle being turned down.
- **Body-image pressure points:** Which physical standards (aging, weight, fitness) carry the most internalized pressure, and how that pressure is typically talked about (or not) out loud.
- **Validation sources:** How much of one's sense of success is tied to external recognition versus internal/relational satisfaction — a real difference in what "feeling good about today" is anchored to.
- **Boundary-setting instinct:** A conditioned pull toward prioritizing others' comfort over one's own limits, versus a more readily available "no."
- **Handling microaggressions:** Practiced, often exhausting strategies for responding to casual gendered assumptions or bias in the moment — deciding in real time whether to correct, let it go, or address it later.
- **Integration goal:** The end state a character coached through all of the above is working toward — these patterns read as genuinely natural and internalized, not as a checklist being visibly performed. A scene showing a character still visibly "running the list" in their head is showing an earlier stage of the Competency Timeline, not the end state.

### Common Outsider Tells — Gender Socialization Crossover

- Performing a category correctly in isolation (e.g., getting the posture right) while missing that these patterns operate as a *cluster* — a character who nails vocal pitch but keeps a wide-elbowed, space-claiming stance sends mixed signals that read as "off" even to observers who can't articulate why.
- Treating the whole list as a checklist to consciously run through in every interaction, rather than letting some patterns become unconscious over story-time while others remain effortful — see the Integration goal note above and Module 4's Adaptation Timeline for how to log that progression.
- Missing that these patterns shift by context — a character may successfully code-switch in a formal work setting but revert under stress, fatigue, or with old friends who knew them before, which is a realistic and usable story beat rather than an inconsistency to avoid.

## Axis: Life-Stage Milestones & Events (Female-Coded Life Course)

*Unlike the routine-based axes above, this axis is a lookup of events that may arise as plot points — a milestone, obligation, or life circumstance a character living as a woman of a given age might need to navigate on-page. Use it when a story introduces one of these events to ground what it typically involves (logistics, social expectations, emotional weight). Whether a specific character has already been through an event, is currently facing it, or is inexperienced with it is a per-story decision tracked in that story's own instance file.*

### Childhood & Early Socialization (Roughly Ages 1–12)

- Early friendship-group formation and playground social dynamics — inclusion/exclusion rituals, "secret clubs," shifting best-friend pairings.
- Receiving gendered toys, clothing, and room decor from a young age, and any story beat involving a character noticing or questioning that conditioning.
- Early safety-conscious warnings from adults (stranger danger, public safety rules) as a formative, repeated experience rather than a single conversation.
- Early modesty conditioning — unwritten rules about body coverage, posture, and public self-presentation, learned well before puberty.
- A first major childhood friendship betrayal or falling-out, often a formative emotional event even decades later.

### Adolescence & Puberty (Roughly Ages 13–19)

- **Menarche (first period):** A major physical/emotional milestone; a plot-relevant event if a story needs a character navigating menstruation for the first time (supplies, timing, discomfort, and often secrecy or embarrassment).
- **Breast development and first bra fitting:** A milestone with its own logistics (shopping, sizing, self-consciousness) distinct from puberty generally.
- Secondary puberty changes — body hair growth, skin changes, growth spurts, hormonal mood shifts — as an ongoing backdrop rather than a single event.
- First crush/infatuation and first romantic rejection, as emotionally significant even when objectively minor.
- Transition to middle/high school — new locker rooms, new academic structure, expanded and reshuffled social circles.
- First paid part-time job (babysitting, retail, etc.) as an early independence/responsibility milestone.
- First encounters with street harassment (catcalling, unwanted public comments) and the resulting habit-formation — altered walking routes, keys held defensively, environmental scanning — that often begins here and persists into adulthood.
- Prom or a major school formal — dress selection, date logistics, and the social weight the event carries.
- Getting a driver's license as a mobility/independence milestone.
- Body image internalization — first real confrontation with beauty standards, diet culture, and media body portrayals, often a lasting reference point.
- High school graduation as a closing-of-a-chapter milestone.
- First major assertion of autonomy — clothing, lifestyle, or belief choices made independent of parental oversight, often the first real friction point with parents.

### Young Adulthood & Early Independence (Roughly Ages 20–29)

- Leaving the family home (college, work, independent living) as a permanent shift in family dynamics, not just a logistical move.
- Completing higher education, a trade program, or certification.
- Entering the workforce and navigating office culture and hierarchy for the first time.
- Encountering the gender pay gap, being talked over professionally, or hitting negotiation friction — a realistic professional-setting plot beat.
- Signing a first independent lease and managing bills/budgeting solo or with roommates.
- Entering a serious long-term relationship with real future-planning stakes.
- A first major heartbreak or the end of a significant adult relationship.
- Developing strategies for workplace microaggressions or casual sexism.
- Establishing financial independence — credit, bank accounts, taxes handled solo for the first time.
- A first major solo trip — new city or country navigated entirely independently.
- Roommate conflict and the negotiation of shared living boundaries.
- Forming deep adult friendships that are expected to outlast career changes and moves.
- Losing a grandparent or elder as a first adult experience of family loss.
- Autonomous decisions around contraception and reproductive health.
- Career burnout from balancing early-career demands with personal wellbeing.
- Actively defining personal values/boundaries distinct from family-of-origin expectations.
- Relocating to a new city away from an existing support network.
- A first major medical procedure navigated as an independent adult patient.
- Learning practical domestic skills (cooking, basic home/car maintenance) as an ongoing competency build.
- Settling into a more mature, personally authentic sense of style and self-presentation.

### Adulthood, Partnership & Family Building (Roughly Ages 30–49)

- Marriage or a long-term committed partnership ceremony.
- The decision of whether to have children, and the weight of that decision either direction.
- Pregnancy and childbirth as a major physical/psychological transformation.
- Early motherhood — sleep deprivation, identity shift, round-the-clock infant care.
- Balancing career ambition against caregiving expectations ("motherhood penalty" friction).
- Adopting a pet as a first shared-responsibility milestone in a household.
- Buying a first home and navigating a mortgage.
- Pregnancy loss or fertility struggles, including miscarriage or fertility treatment, as a significant and often private emotional event.
- Divorce or the end of a marriage, with its legal/financial/emotional fallout.
- Single parenting and its distinct day-to-day logistics.
- Reaching a career peak, leadership promotion, or founding a business.
- Becoming a caregiver for aging parents.
- Losing a parent — a major generational-shift grief event.
- Long-term financial planning — investments, retirement accounts, portfolio decisions.
- Becoming a stepmother or navigating a blended family after separation.
- A mid-career pivot or return to school/retraining later in life.
- Parenting a child with a long-term illness or special needs, including medical advocacy.
- Launching adult children into college, careers, or independent living.
- The "empty nest" adjustment after decades of active parenting.
- Receiving and managing a chronic health diagnosis.
- Increased community/civic involvement — local leadership, volunteering, activism.
- Narrowing one's social circle to a smaller, more reliable core group.
- The "sandwich generation" squeeze — simultaneously supporting children and aging parents.
- A milestone wedding anniversary (10th, 15th, 20th, etc.).
- Home renovation or long-term stabilization of a living space.

### Midlife & Hormonal Transitions (Roughly Ages 50–64)

- Perimenopause onset — early physiological/emotional shifts.
- Ongoing hormonal symptom management — hot flashes, sleep disruption, mood changes, cognitive shifts ("brain fog").
- Menopause confirmation (12 consecutive months without a period) as a defined milestone closing out reproductive years.
- Becoming a grandmother or taking on an elder-mentor role for younger generations.
- A midlife identity reevaluation — reflecting on past choices and reconsidering the shape of the years ahead.
- Visibly aging — graying hair, changing skin texture, metabolism shifts — and the character's own relationship to that change.
- Losing friends/peers as mortality becomes a more present reality within one's own age cohort.
- Finalizing retirement planning.
- Reclaiming personal time for hobbies/passions after decades of caregiving or career focus.
- Losing the final surviving parent, becoming the family's senior generation.
- Attending adult children's major milestones — weddings, career achievements, grandchildren.
- Committing to routine preventive health screenings (mammograms, bone density scans, etc.).
- Downsizing out of a family home into a smaller space.
- Extended leisure travel now that time/finances allow.
- Taking up a new skill or craft with dedicated focus.

### Later Life & Legacy (Ages 65+)

- Formal retirement from the workforce.
- Qualifying for senior benefits/Medicare-equivalent programs.
- Losing a spouse or life partner — the grief and practical realities of widowhood.
- Adapting living space, driving habits, or routines to mobility changes.
- Writing a will and handling estate planning, medical directives.
- Becoming a great-grandmother.
- Sorting through decades of photos, letters, and mementos.
- Distributing treasured possessions to family, or otherwise simplifying belongings.
- Establishing formal healthcare and end-of-life wishes.
- Adjusting to reliance on assistive devices or home health support.
- Finding comfort in faith, nature, or a sense of legacy as life narrows toward its final chapters.
- Celebrating a major milestone birthday (75th, 80th, 90th) surrounded by family.
- Mentoring younger relatives — passing down recipes, family history, personal wisdom.
- Recording personal stories/family lore for posterity.
- Approaching the end of life with a sense of completion, surrounded by accumulated history and loved ones.

*This axis is a lookup for grounding plot-relevant events, not a checklist a character needs to "complete." A story may draw on one entry in isolation without implicating the rest of the life course. Cross-reference Module 2 (Identity & Memory Continuity Tracker) when a swapped or changed character's plausible life history is in question — this axis supplies what such a history would typically include.*
