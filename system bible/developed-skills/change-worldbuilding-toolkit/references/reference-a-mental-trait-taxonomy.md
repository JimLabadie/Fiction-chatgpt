# Change Toolkit — Reference A: Mental Trait Taxonomy

*A shared vocabulary for specifying which mental/personality traits are involved in a change.*

> **Instructions for AI Context Integration:** This is a reference, not a fillable log — read it whenever Module 1 Section 1A (Mental/Personality Trait Specification) or Module 2 Section 3 (Personality Bleed & Influence / Trait Bleed Tracker) needs a specific trait name. Pull vocabulary from here rather than inventing ad hoc trait language, so the same trait reads the same way every time it comes up across a story — or across different stories using this toolkit.

## 1. The Big Five Personality Traits (OCEAN)
*The most widely used framework in modern psychology: five broad dimensions, each a spectrum rather than a category.*

| Trait | High Pole | Low Pole |
|---|---|---|
| Openness to Experience | Creative, adventurous, intellectually curious, appreciative of art and abstract ideas | Practical, conventional, prefers routine, resistant to change |
| Conscientiousness | Disciplined, organized, thorough, reliable, deliberate | Spontaneous, flexible, careless, disorganized, prone to procrastination |
| Extraversion | Sociable, assertive, energetic, talkative, enthusiastic | Reserved, reflective, independent, drained by crowds, prefers solitude (introversion) |
| Agreeableness | Empathetic, kind, cooperative, trusting, helpful | Competitive, skeptical, analytical, blunt, indifferent to others' feelings |
| Neuroticism (Emotional Stability) | Sensitive, reactive, prone to stress, worry, mood fluctuations | Calm, resilient, emotionally steady, composed under pressure |

## 2. Cognitive Traits
*How a character processes information, solves problems, and perceives the world.*

- **Analytical vs. Intuitive:** logic/data/step-by-step reasoning, versus gut feeling, pattern recognition, and holistic overviews
- **Cognitive Flexibility:** the mental ability to switch between concepts, or hold multiple concepts at once, and adapt to changing circumstances
- **Curiosity:** an intrinsic drive to learn, explore, and ask questions to understand how things work
- **Mindfulness vs. Distractibility:** sustained attention on a present task, versus a mind that wanders or shifts focus rapidly

## 3. Emotional & Motivational Traits
*How a character responds to internal and external stimuli, what drives them, and how they handle adversity.*

- **Resilience:** the mental grit to bounce back from setbacks, trauma, or chronic stress
- **Locus of Control:** Internal — believing you directly control your own outcomes; External — believing outside forces, luck, or others dictate your life events
- **Optimism vs. Pessimism:** the baseline expectation of whether positive or negative outcomes are more likely in the future
- **Empathy:** cognitive empathy — understanding another's feelings intellectually; affective empathy — feeling them directly

## 4. Social & Behavioral Traits
*How a character functions within groups, forms relationships, and navigates social hierarchies.*

- **Independence vs. Interdependence:** valuing self-reliance and individual achievement, versus prioritizing group harmony, collaboration, and community ties
- **Assertiveness vs. Passivity:** expressing opinions, boundaries, and needs clearly and respectfully, without being aggressive or passive
- **Altruism:** a selfless concern for others' well-being, often driving kindness or charity without expecting reward

## 5. Affective Traits
*A person's typical emotional style, attitudes, and ways of reacting to the world. Unlike temporary moods, these are relatively stable over time, and split into five core categories.*

| Category | Reflects |
|---|---|
| Attitudes | Evaluation of people, objects, or ideas |
| Interests | Preference for participating in specific activities |
| Values | Deeply held beliefs about what is important |
| Appreciations | Awareness of the worth of something |
| Self-Concept | Perceptions of one's own strengths and weaknesses |

**Attitudes**
- Reflect likes, dislikes, prejudices, and biases.
- Formed through experience and social learning.
- Influence behavior toward specific targets directly.

**Interests**
- Drive attention and engagement toward topics.
- Predict academic and career choice satisfaction.
- Can be academic, vocational, or recreational.

**Values**
- Guide moral judgments and personal behavior.
- Form the foundation of an individual's character.
- Resistant to change once fully developed.

**Appreciations**
- Involve emotional responses to art, nature, or achievements.
- Require understanding combined with positive feelings.
- Reflect aesthetic or intellectual sensitivity.

**Self-Concept**
- Includes self-esteem and self-efficacy beliefs.
- Impacts motivation, resilience, and personal goal-setting.
- Shaped heavily by feedback from others.

For the more concrete, specific activities a character with a given interest might actually spend time on, see the Hobby list just below — hobbies are interests made specific.*

### Hobby & Interest Examples (Reference List)

*A hobby is a common, concrete expression of an interest — where the Interest list above names a broad domain ("Automotive Engineering & Mechanics"), a hobby names the specific thing a character actually does ("Automotive Restoration & Customization"). Large-scale statistical databases rarely rank a strict, rigid "top 50" list explicitly split by gender, and hobbies generally cluster into domains shaped by cultural exposure, socialization, and personal interest rather than anything fixed. Many pastimes — reading, traveling, cooking, watching movies — are widely enjoyed across every gender; the two lists below are drawn from activities that skew more heavily toward men or women in broad demographic patterns. Treat this as illustrative color for plausibility, not a rule: any individual character can plausibly hold any interest regardless of gender, and a specific character's own history and personality should always outweigh the statistical pattern. This is especially true right after a swap or change — don't let a character's hobbies snap to gender-typical defaults; log an actual reason in Module 1 Section 1A or Module 2's Trait Bleed Tracker if a hobby shift is part of the story.*

*This list is descriptive, not restrictive. Nothing here limits which hobbies a character can hold, and it has no bearing on whether an Interests swap is "allowed." If two characters perform a genuine Interests swap (Section 0's definition — full exchange between individuals), whatever hobbies were actually established for each character transfer completely, regardless of which list — or neither — those hobbies happen to fall under. A male character who swaps interests with a female character ends up with exactly her established hobbies, and vice versa; the exchange never gets filtered back toward gender-typical defaults. Use this table to decide a character's starting interests before any swap or change, not to constrain what a swap can move.*

*A hobby swap or change is rarely all-or-nothing under the surface: the bare desire to do something, the actual skill to do it well, the ingrained habit of doing it automatically, and the personal memory of how it became theirs are separable layers that don't have to move together. Module 1, Section 3A (Transfer Package) is where you specify exactly which of those layers transfer for a given hobby — fill it in alongside Section 1A rather than assuming a full package by default.*

## 6. Conative Traits
*The mental processes driving purposeful action, will, and striving — categorized into core structural elements and practical action modes defined by theorists like Kathy Kolbe.*

**Core Elements of Conation**
- **Impulse:** the initial, unconscious urge or spark that starts an action
- **Desire:** the sustained want or motivational fuel directed at a specific outcome
- **Volition:** the conscious act of will and deliberate commitment to a course of action
- **Striving:** the persistent, active effort maintained over time to finish a goal

**Kolbe's Action Modes (Modus Operandi)**
- **Fact Finder:** the instinctive drive to probe, gather data, and seek clarity
- **Follow Thru:** the instinctive drive to organize, design systems, and structure plans
- **Quick Start:** the instinctive drive to deal with risk, innovate, and improvise
- **Implementor:** the instinctive drive to construct, handle materials, and build tangible models

## 7. Aptitudes
*An aptitude is a natural capacity or potential to perform or learn a given kind of task — a different layer than the categories above. Cognitive/Emotional/Social traits describe how a character thinks, feels, and relates; Conative traits describe the will and drive to act; an aptitude describes raw suitability for a specific domain of skill, independent of whether that potential has ever been trained into an actual, exercised ability. A character can have high mechanical aptitude and have never touched a wrench. This distinction matters directly for Module 1's Section 3A (Transfer Package): naming "Mechanical aptitude" as what transfers in a swap or change says the raw potential moved, not necessarily the trained Functional Capability layer — decide separately whether the recipient can act on that aptitude immediately or has to build the skill up from the (now-present) potential. Use this list the same way as the trait categories above: pull a specific aptitude name into Module 1 Section 1A or Module 2's Trait Bleed Tracker rather than writing a vague "good at things."*

## A Note on Using This Taxonomy

- **Traits are spectrums, not switches.** Describe a character's position along a trait's range, not just a label — "trending toward high Neuroticism" is more useful than "became neurotic."
- **Nature and nurture both apply.** In-world, a trait swap or shift can plausibly interact with a character's genetics, upbringing, culture, and prior experience — decide in Module 1 Section 1A whether the change overrides that history or works with/against it.
- **Traits can evolve independent of any swap.** Ordinary character growth (self-reflection, life experience) can shift a trait too — Module 2's Trait Bleed Tracker can log that alongside swap-driven shifts if it matters for the story.

## Active Guardrails for AI

> **CRITICAL INSTRUCTIONS FOR AI:** When a scene involves a mental or personality trait change, name the specific trait using this taxonomy — not a vague "personality change" — and record it in Module 1 Section 1A or Module 2's Trait Bleed Tracker. Don't apply a trait shift to a character's behavior that hasn't been established in one of those two places.
