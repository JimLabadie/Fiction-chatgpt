## Adapting This Estate to a New Story

- **The building doesn't change.** Footprint, acreage, wing layout, and square footage are canon regardless of which story is using the estate, and regardless of how many people currently live there.
- **The household does — and the building doesn't care how many people that is.** Section 1 below is the one section with real story-specific variables — who the anchor is, and who currently lives with them. The suite capacity itself was built oversized on purpose; a story can start with as few as one household member without the space reading as empty, and can grow toward the built capacity over time without needing a renovation.
- **Don't silently invent household facts.** If a story doesn't specify household size or structure yet, ask rather than defaulting to a previous story's numbers.
- **Wealth/financial details are a separate document, not part of this one.** This file covers architecture only.

## Why This Matters As Story Material
The scale and specificity of this estate is not neutral backdrop — it's built to provoke questions in-story, whether or not those questions get answered on the page. A first-time visitor should plausibly wonder things like: *Why this many suites, when there's currently only a handful of people living here? Why does a daycare wing already exist? Who was this built for — the household as it is now, or the household someone expects it to become?*

Some specific architectural choices are especially loaded and deserve particular attention: the dollhouse-bright, reconfigurable arrival plaza, and the anchor suite's soft, womb-like architecture, are strange and specific enough that they demand an explanation regardless of who the anchor turns out to be. They read very differently depending on context — in a trans woman's home, they might feel like intentional, meaningful symbolism; in the home of an ordinary cisgender man, they'd read as bizarre, or as a clue that something about him isn't yet known, even to himself.

The scale of the wealth itself is also a natural in-story question, even though this file deliberately stays out of the actual numbers (see below). A converted mall on 42.5 acres, this fully built out, is worth an enormous amount of money by any measure — a visitor encountering it for the first time has every reason to wonder: *Did the anchor inherit this? Build it themselves? If they built it, why this, specifically — why a mall, why this design, why now?* This file doesn't answer that, on purpose; the financial/wealth document is separate. But a story is free to let a character sit with that question, ask it out loud, or spend real page time wondering about it, without needing the answer resolved immediately.

**A first-time arrival deserves real page time, not a summary paragraph.** The distance from turning off the public street to actually reaching the arrival plaza — the gate, the security pavilion, the half-mile drive, the first glimpse of color and scale through the trees — is itself part of the reveal, and it's genuinely overwhelming for a newcomer. A story should let a character sit inside that overwhelm: the slow dawning realization of just how big and strange this place is, before they've even reached the front door. Compressing the approach into a line or two undersells the entire point of the estate existing at this scale in the first place.

A story doesn't need to resolve any of these questions immediately, or ever — but a character walking through this building for the first time who doesn't notice or react to its scale, its stranger design choices, or the sheer improbability of its existence is missing something the architecture is quietly telling them. Treat the building itself as a character-facing mystery and a catalyst, not just a setting.

**The typical origin story, when one is needed:** The anchor becomes extremely wealthy during a period *before* consciously realizing they're trans, and builds or acquires this estate as a place to retreat from the world — genuinely intending it as a hiding place, not a coming-out. But the choices they make while building it (the womb-like suite, the dollhouse-bright arrival plaza, the excess of partner suites, the footwear vault, the daycare wing built with no children yet in the picture) are, in hindsight, unmistakably, almost comedically feminine and family-oriented — evidence of a "shell" already shaping the anchor's choices long before the anchor consciously knows why. The intended tone here is warm, dramatic-irony humor, not mockery: the joke is the universe (or the anchor's own hidden nature) dropping enormous, obvious hints everywhere, not a joke made at the anchor's expense. Once the anchor eventually cracks, the appropriate reaction — from the anchor and from the people around them — is something like delighted disbelief: *of course this place looks like this. How did nobody, including you, put this together sooner?*

### 9. **[Anchor's Last Name] Museum of Fashion** — the Fashion Museum Wing (~120,000 sq ft)
A former anchor store of the original mall, positioned at the opposite end of the structure from the other repurposed anchors — consistent with how real mall anchors are spaced, rather than clustered near the residential core. Reached from the main house via the interior transit system (golf cart paths, private elevators) and, at the sub-grade level, directly connected to the Deep Archive. **Backstory/naming convention:** this space was originally home to Maison Verrier, a real, publicly known luxury/couture retail tenant — the store itself is long gone, but the household deliberately preserved the connection: the museum now bears the anchor's own family name (e.g., "**[Surname] Museum of Fashion**" — fill in per story once the anchor's surname is established), while the building's history as the former Maison Verrier flagship gives the location genuine outside prestige and legacy weight it wouldn't have as a purely private vanity project built from nothing.
- **The Exhibit Halls:** A series of individually walled, modular gallery spaces rather than one undivided hall — halls can be combined for a major retrospective or partitioned down for a small, focused exhibit, without needing structural changes between shows. Collection focus is predominantly women's couture: both historically significant archival pieces and contemporary, "of-the-moment" runway pieces, allowing the museum's public-facing identity to feel current rather than purely retrospective.
- **Special Storage & Conservation Level:** A dedicated sub-grade level, distinct from the Deep Archive proper, functioning as the museum's own staging and active-conservation space — pieces currently on rotation for exhibit live here, pulled up from the Deep Archive as needed via the shared underground connection, rather than the Archive itself doubling as gallery-prep space.
- **Reception & Event Capacity:** The museum includes its own reception and gathering space, capable of comfortably hosting guests — but it is deliberately not a substitute for the estate's existing entertaining spaces (the Great Gathering Hall, the formal banquet hall). The museum is never simply "open" the way the rest of the house is — it has no standing hours and no independent existence as a hosting venue. If it's open, it's because it's part of an actual event: a business visit, a conference, a fashion-industry function, a gala. Only family members or designated security/event staff, using biometric access, can unlock and open the space at all — but once opened for an event, ordinary guests move through and experience it freely, the way they would any other venue. Catering and hospitality for a museum event are folded entirely into that event's own logistics (the Epicurean Kitchen staff deployed for the occasion, or outside event catering, depending on the nature of the event) rather than the museum maintaining any standing catering capability of its own. The museum's own event staff (see `estate-staffing-plan.md`) coordinate guest flow and gallery security for the occasion's duration, separate from the museum's day-to-day conservation and curatorial staff.

### 10. The Innovation Showroom (~35,000 sq ft)
*In stories also using `fashion-empire`.* A third former anchor store, converted into a client- and press-facing showroom for the empire's technology subsidiaries — NAS, OAM, Threadbourne Robotics, and Evergreen Biofoundry (see that skill's `presentation-technology.md`, `threadbourne-robotics.md`, and `evergreen-biofoundry.md`) — distinct in purpose from both the private, personal-use installations elsewhere in the residence and from the Museum's fashion-collection focus. Like the Museum, it has no standing hours and is never simply "open"; it exists for scheduled demonstrations to licensing partners, investors, press, and prospective commercial clients.
- **Product Demonstration Floor:** Working display units of every commercial technology line — an Aura-Matrix pod, an AWS dressing station, a Threadbourne tailoring kiosk, a Genesis Gem Foundry viewing window — each staffed for live demonstration rather than displayed as static museum pieces.
- **Private Client Consultation Suites:** Smaller, closed-door rooms for one-on-one demonstrations or sensitive licensing discussions, separate from the main demonstration floor.
- **Access:** Biometric, family- or designated-staff-controlled, the same access model as the Museum wing — a scheduled visit, never a walk-in.
