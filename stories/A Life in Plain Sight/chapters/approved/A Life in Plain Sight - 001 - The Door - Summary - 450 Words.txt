Steve is cooking dinner when his older sister Brittney announces that he is going to a lesbian bar. He refuses. Brittney refuses his refusal. Her wife Liz contributes sarcasm, stolen food, and the confidence of someone thoroughly at home in a family that argues without threatening its bonds.

Steve sees little wrong with his life. He has a good software engineering job, a comfortable apartment, hobbies, and an appetite for technical problems that can unexpectedly lead to experiments with chicken marinades. Brittney points out that work, shopping, and visiting relatives do not amount to much of a social life. Beneath the teasing, her concern is sincere.

Blush strikes Steve as an especially illogical destination. He likes women, but the women there are lesbians. Brittney insists that he can simply have a drink and enjoy himself. She and Liz also know people who visit the bar, although neither provides the useful details Steve requests.

Eventually he agrees to one drink. His intended outcome is straightforward: demonstrate that the suggestion was foolish, return home, and retire Brittney’s campaign. When she tells him she is not trying to fix him, a brief moment of tenderness interrupts the argument before ordinary family chaos resumes.

Alone afterward, Steve struggles with what to wear. His small, fine-boned body has always led strangers to perceive him as a woman. Shirts that actually fit follow his waist; slim jeans and narrow boots suit his proportions better than heavier alternatives. He resents the impression these practical choices create and makes familiar, mostly ineffective attempts to compensate.

At Blush, his anticipated confrontation never happens. The entrance is welcoming, the room contains a variety of women enjoying their evening, and nobody challenges his presence. A passing patron compliments his shirt. Rather than correcting an initial impression, the room simply accepts him and continues.

Steve orders his beer, preparing to complete the agreed obligation. Nearby, five women are trading affectionate insults about Sales, facts, and revenue. He finds their argument funny despite his determination to remain uninvolved.

Chloe approaches him at the bar. Beautiful, direct, and dryly amused, she draws him into conversation and introduces the possibility of joining her friends. Her invitation carries no pressure. Steve realizes that promising one drink did not require drinking it alone.

At the table, Nat loudly welcomes the tiny engineer, his name produces a suspicious pause, and Maddie abruptly asks about ghosts. Sofia introduces herself while Tori attempts to contain the conversational disorder.

Within seconds, Steve is sparring with Nat, Tori is demanding definitions, Maddie’s winged horse has become a tax accountant named Dennis, and Sofia is laughing. Chloe watches him settle into their company. Steve temporarily forgets the exit, finding himself unexpectedly at ease.
