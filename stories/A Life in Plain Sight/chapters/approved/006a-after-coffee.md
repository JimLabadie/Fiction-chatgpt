# Chapter Six-A

## After Coffee

> Recovery note: The exact prose of the previously drafted Chapter 006 art-collective continuation is not available in the current repository state. This file preserves the accepted scene beats without pretending to reconstruct the missing prose verbatim. It should remain a recovery bridge until the original text is recovered or Jim explicitly approves a reconstruction.

At approximately 4:18 PM Sunday, Steve realizes he and Chloe have been at coffee for more than two hours. Neither wants the time together to end.

Steve remembers a feminine/women’s art collective in St. Claire. He knows about it because of a zoning dispute involving mixed retail/studio occupancy and prior determinations. He initially packages the suggestion as something Maddie might like. Chloe notices the procedural evasion and makes him actually ask her to go with him. Steve admits he wants to go; they go together.

The collective is a brick storefront between a bakery and an expensive soap shop, with a hand-painted sign and a warm, lived-in atmosphere rather than a whispery museum feeling. It contains paintings, photography, textiles, ceramics, jewelry, and clothing work. Steve becomes absorbed in construction details: a hidden structural join inside embroidery, textile tension, a jewelry clasp, glaze and thermal expansion. Chloe enjoys watching him enjoy himself.

In the back room, Steve stops at a painting of five women getting ready together in a bedroom. One applies mascara, one does another woman’s hair, one in a slip is laughing; dresses hang on the closet door, shoes are everywhere, along with a curling iron, bracelets, and a glass. Nobody in the painting looks at the viewer. The image is ordinary feminine belonging, not explicit trans imagery and not an identity-revelation device.

The painting hits Steve unexpectedly hard. He initially retreats into technical analysis—light, depth, reflected color, composition—but his breath catches and his eyes become wet. He says, “I don’t know what the fuck is wrong with me.” Chloe does not interpret, diagnose, invoke Brittney’s hypothesis, or tell him what the reaction means. She asks whether he wants to stay or go outside. Steve chooses to stay.

Steve calls the reaction stupid. Chloe refuses to reassure him with an interpretation she does not have: she does not know what he is feeling yet, and neither does he, so calling it stupid is premature. She offers her hand palm-up rather than taking his. Steve takes it.

Steve says, “It feels familiar.” Chloe asks whether he has seen something like it before. He says no. When she accepts that, he says, “That’s the problem.” Chloe still does not explain it for him. She simply stays.

Eventually Steve says they can keep going. They walk through the collective hand in hand. Steve notices after roughly six steps that they are still holding hands and does not let go.

Later Chloe is drawn to a small, useless ceramic piece: shallow asymmetrical pale clay, glazed inside, with a pattern that changes by viewing angle. She picks it up, reads the card, and puts it back. While Chloe speaks with a photographer, Steve buys it for eighteen dollars.

Chloe asks why. Steve first says it was eighteen dollars. When she points out that is not the point, he admits he knows—that is why he mentioned the price second. His real answer is: “Because you liked it.” The beat quietly echoes Chloe’s permission-to-want theme without either character explaining the theme aloud.

Chloe takes Steve’s hand again, directly this time. He leaves it there.

They leave the collective a little after 6 PM and return to Steve’s pink Mini. They acknowledge that this was a very long coffee. Steve tells Chloe he had fun; Chloe answers that she did too, echoing their earlier text exchange.

Chloe steps closer and waits, preserving Steve’s choice. Steve understands and leans forward himself; Chloe meets him. Their first kiss is soft, brief, and warm. It is not a revelation or cosmic event.

Steve says, “That was not coffee.”

Chloe answers, “There was coffee.”

Steve says, “Right.”

This was technically true.
