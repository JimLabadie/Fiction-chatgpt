# Chapter Four — So Not a Date

Jim discovered Amour Noir by nearly walking past it.

Kate did not.

She slowed half a step, which was enough for Jim to notice because the heels she had been wearing all night had a very specific rhythm against the sidewalk. Four and a half inches of polished confidence: click, click, click.

Then one beat missing.

Jim looked over.

Kate was looking through a dark window washed in candlelight.

Gold lettering on the glass read AMOUR NOIR.

Jim followed her gaze inside.

Small tables. White linen. Wine glasses. Candles. Women leaning toward each other across intimate little islands of light. A woman near the back had both hands wrapped around another woman's hand while they talked. At another table, somebody had apparently dressed for the evening with the seriousness of a diplomatic summit.

Jim looked at Kate.

Kate looked at Jim.

“No.”

“I didn't say anything.”

“You made a face.”

“I have a face.”

“You made a specific face.”

“What face?”

“The one where you know something I don't.”

Kate shifted the pink flamingo lamp under her arm.

Jim pointed at the restaurant.

“What is this?”

“A restaurant.”

“Bullshit.”

“A very good restaurant.”

“That wasn't the question.”

“You said you were hungry.”

“I am.”

“So am I.”

Jim looked through the glass again.

A server crossed the room in a fitted black dress. Candlelight flashed against stemware. Somewhere inside, a woman laughed and covered her mouth with one hand while the woman across from her smiled like she had caused it on purpose.

Jim looked back at Kate.

“This is a date restaurant.”

Kate's mouth twitched.

“It serves food.”

“Funeral homes have chairs. That doesn't make them furniture stores.”

“I don't know what you want from me.”

“The truth.”

“The truth is I'm hungry.”

“And?”

“And you're hungry.”

“And?”

“And we're standing outside a restaurant.”

“And?”

Kate smiled.

Jim narrowed his eyes.

“Oh, fuck you.”

“I still haven't said anything.”

“You don't have to. Your hair is smug.”

Kate's hand went automatically to the extraordinary fall of hair over one shoulder.

“My hair is never smug.”

“It knows what it did.”

“Jim.”

“Kate.”

They stared at each other.

The flamingo lamp stared at both of them.

Kate sighed.

“Amour Noir is known for being romantic.”

“How romantic?”

“Romantic.”

“Scale of one to ten.”

“I don't know.”

“Kate.”

“Eleven.”

Jim closed his eyes.

Kate bit the inside of her cheek.

“First dates?”

“Yes.”

“Anniversaries?”

“Yes.”

“Proposals?”

Kate said nothing.

Jim opened his eyes.

“Proposals?”

“It has happened.”

“Oh, absolutely not.”

He turned.

Kate caught the back of his new flannel.

Jim stopped.

Her fingers were curled into the fabric between his shoulder blades.

He looked over his shoulder.

Kate looked at her own hand.

Then at him.

“We need food.”

“There are other restaurants.”

“Not this close.”

“You don't know that.”

“I don't live here.”

“Neither do I.”

“Exactly.”

“That argument doesn't even—”

“Jim.”

“What?”

“Food isn't a date.”

He stared at her.

That was his line.

Kate knew it.

Jim knew she knew it.

Her smile spread slowly.

“People eat food with people they aren't dating all the time,” she said.

“You're a thief.”

“Constantly.”

“Intellectual property theft.”

“We could eat food.”

“Platonically?”

“Extremely.”

Jim looked through the window again.

Then down at himself.

The flannel Kate had made him try on sat differently now that he'd stopped thinking about it. The boots she'd bought him were comfortable enough to be insulting. The carabiner clipped to his belt loop made a soft metallic tick when he moved.

He had looked in the thrift-store mirror and, for once, liked the joke his body seemed to be telling.

Kate was looking at him again.

Not at the restaurant.

At him.

Jim caught her.

“What?”

Kate's eyes snapped upward.

“Nothing.”

“No. That was something.”

“I was checking the flannel.”

“For structural damage?”

“Exactly.”

Jim smiled.

Kate hated how well it worked on her.

The shirt should not have worked. That was the first problem.

Jim was tiny. Five-six, a hundred and five pounds, built with none of the bulk the shirt seemed to imply from a hanger. On him it did something else entirely. The soft plaid made his narrow frame look intentional rather than apologetic. The boots grounded him. The stupid carabiner at his hip should have been parody.

Instead it looked—

Kate's eyes dropped again.

Jim's eyebrow rose.

“Caught you.”

“Shut up.”

“You were looking.”

“At the lamp.”

“The lamp is on your side.”

“Peripheral vision.”

“You looked at my ass.”

Kate's jaw dropped.

Jim grinned.

“Oh, this is fantastic.”

“I did not look at your ass.”

“You were conducting an inspection.”

“Your shirt.”

“Very close to my ass.”

“Anatomically adjacent is not legally identical.”

Jim barked out that ugly, real laugh she liked.

And there it was again.

That was the second problem.

The more comfortable he got, the hotter he got.

Not theoretically.

Not in the careful, compassionate, identity-is-complicated way Kate's brain kept trying to make respectable.

Hot.

Abso-fucking-lutely hot.

The flannel. The boots. The carabiner. His small frame. The mischievous little swagger he'd acquired after deciding he'd won lesbianism. The fact that he had stopped asking permission from the room before he laughed.

Kate had spent years becoming very good at deliberate femininity. She understood presentation as craft, language, pleasure, armor, declaration.

Jim had apparently stumbled into a dialect twenty minutes ago and was already flirting in it.

It was infuriating.

“Fine,” she said.

Jim blinked. “Fine what?”

“We'll ask if they have a table.”

His grin widened.

“Not a date.”

“Not remotely.”

“Because I don't want there to be confusion.”

“God forbid.”

“And if somebody proposes, we're leaving.”

Kate looked at him.

“Somebody?”

“Anybody.”

“Very sensible.”

“Thank you.”

She opened the door.

Jim gestured grandly.

“After you.”

Kate lifted the flamingo lamp.

“I am carrying your trash.”

“Art.”

“Trash.”

“Charitable art.”

“Move.”

They went inside.

The first thing Jim noticed was how quiet it wasn't.

Amour Noir had conversation everywhere, but none of it fought for the room. Twenty-six seats arranged in small tables, candlelight, dark wood, glass, the soft movement of people who had been given permission to take their time. The air carried wine, warm bread, butter, herbs, something richer from the kitchen. Service moved around the room without making the room feel serviced.

The second thing Jim noticed was that Kate had not exaggerated.

This place was gay as hell.

Not gay-bar gay.

Courting gay.

Women in dresses. Women in suits. A silver-haired butch in a dark tailored jacket leaning across a table toward a femme whose fingers rested around a wineglass. Two younger women sharing something from one plate. A pair near the wall sitting close enough that their knees stayed touching.

Jim stopped just inside.

Kate nearly bumped him.

“Oh,” he said.

“Mm-hm.”

“This is criminal.”

“You agreed to food.”

“You withheld material information.”

“I said eleven.”

“I thought you were exaggerating.”

“I never exaggerate.”

Jim turned and looked at the flamingo lamp.

Kate followed his gaze.

Neither spoke.

Jim said, “We look insane.”

“We?”

“Fine. You look elegant. I look insane.”

Kate looked him over.

Slowly.

Again.

This time Jim felt it.

Not the quick appraisal from the thrift store. Not teasing.

Her eyes moved from his boots to the line of his jeans, the flannel, the carabiner, his face.

Something warm tightened low in his stomach.

Kate realized what she'd done and looked away.

Jim smiled.

“You keep doing that.”

“Doing what?”

“Nothing.”

“Exactly.”

At the front table, a woman with long wavy auburn hair looked up from a reservation book. She was white, average height when she stood, her silk dress part of a polished professional presentation rather than an attempt to compete with the room. She had the quiet authority of someone who did not need to hurry anybody, including herself.

“Good evening.”

“Hi,” Kate said.

Jim waited.

The woman looked at Kate, then Jim, then the flamingo lamp.

There.

That was usually where it happened.

Not the lamp.

Him.

The tiny recalculation. The eyes returning to his face. The fractional hesitation before a title or pronoun or assumption. Sometimes an apology before anyone had even done anything wrong. Sometimes the opposite: the hard commitment to whatever they'd decided he was.

Jim's shoulders prepared.

The woman said, “Do you have a reservation?”

That was it.

Jim's shoulders forgot what they had been preparing for.

“No,” Kate said. “This was spontaneous.”

“Extremely,” Jim added.

The woman's eyes flicked once to the lamp again.

That, apparently, was the thing requiring explanation.

Jim almost laughed.

She checked the book.

“Friday walk-ins are difficult.”

“We can go somewhere else,” Jim said immediately.

Kate glanced at him.

The woman checked the room, then the book again.

“We had a cancellation. If the two of you don't mind a small table, I can accommodate you.”

Kate smiled. “We don't mind.”

“And there's no need to rush. We don't turn tables aggressively here.”

Jim stared at her.

“You're telling us we can stay?”

A hint of amusement touched her face.

“Yes.”

“I already love this place.”

Kate made a tiny sound.

Jim turned.

“What?”

“Nothing.”

The woman picked up two menus.

Jim said, because apparently he had suffered a head injury, “Just so we're clear, this isn't a date.”

Kate closed her eyes.

The woman's expression remained professionally serene.

“Of course.”

Jim relaxed.

She turned.

Then, over her shoulder:

“Good luck with your proposal.”

Kate stopped breathing.

Jim stared after her.

“What?”

Kate made a strangled noise.

“Our what?”

The woman continued toward the table as if she had not just thrown a grenade behind her.

Jim turned to Kate.

“You heard that.”

Kate pressed her lips together.

“You heard that.”

Her shoulders started shaking.

“Oh, fuck you.”

“I didn't—”

“You are laughing.”

“No.”

“You are visibly vibrating.”

Kate put one hand over her mouth.

Jim pointed toward the front.

“I specifically told her it wasn't a date.”

That did it.

Kate folded around the flamingo lamp.

Jim stared at her.

“This is your fault.”

She tried to speak.

Nothing came out.

“Your restaurant.”

Kate finally managed, “I don't live here.”

“You knew.”

“I knew it was romantic.”

“You knew it had proposal infrastructure.”

“Proposal infrastructure?”

“Yes.”

Kate laughed harder.

The auburn-haired woman stopped beside a two-top and waited with impossible patience.

Jim muttered, “I hate you.”

“No, you don't.”

“Current evidence is mixed.”

They reached the table.

It really was small. Intimate enough that their knees might touch if either forgot to behave. A candle burned between two place settings. The flamingo lamp, once Kate set it carefully beside her chair, looked like it had been arrested for trespassing.

The woman handed them their menus.

“Selene Dupond,” she said. “Pilar will be taking care of you tonight.”

“Thank you,” Kate said.

Jim looked at Selene.

“No proposal.”

Selene inclined her head.

“Of course.”

She walked away.

Kate waited exactly one second.

“Good luck with your proposal.”

Jim pointed at her.

“Don't.”

“I'm supporting you.”

“Don't.”

“In this exciting new chapter.”

“Kate.”

She smiled sweetly.

Jim sat.

At the next table, one of the women glanced over, caught sight of the flamingo lamp, and smiled openly at Jim before turning back to her companion.

Jim blinked.

No second look.

No confusion.

Just shared amusement at the lamp.

He sat down.

Kate watched that happen.

More importantly, she watched what didn't.

His shoulders hadn't risen.

Interesting.

She put the thought away.

For now.

Jim opened the menu.

Kate opened hers.

Three seconds passed.

Jim looked over the top.

“This is still not a date.”

Kate didn't look up.

“You're the one who keeps saying it.”

“Because the environment is hostile.”

“The candle?”

“Judgmental.”

“The wine list?”

“Complicit.”

“The flamingo?”

“Traitor.”

Kate lowered her menu.

The candlelight caught in her hair.

Jim forgot his next line.

That happened enough now to be concerning.

Kate saw it.

Her smile softened.

Jim looked back at the menu.

“Stop.”

“I didn't say anything.”

“Your face did.”

“My face is proprietary.”

A server approached.

Pilar Zamora was a Colombian Latina in her mid-thirties, average height, dark hair swept into a low chignon. Her fitted black dress matched the room's deliberate elegance, and she moved with the unhurried control of someone who had decided years ago that other people's panic did not get to set her pace.

“Good evening. I'm Pilar.”

Her voice was soft and calm.

“Kate.”

“Jim.”

Pilar looked at both of them.

Jim felt the old reflex again.

Wait.

Wait for it.

Nothing.

Pilar's attention moved to the menus.

“Have you been in before?”

“No,” Kate said.

Jim added, “Apparently we came dangerously unprepared.”

Pilar glanced toward Selene.

“Did she frighten you?”

“She wished us luck with our proposal.”

Pilar's mouth twitched.

Kate said, “He's very upset.”

“I stated a fact.”

“Repeatedly.”

Pilar looked at the flamingo lamp.

“And this?”

Jim brightened.

“Art.”

“Evidence,” Kate said.

Pilar looked from one to the other.

For the first time, her professional expression broke.

Not much.

Enough for a genuine smile.

“I see.”

“No, you don't,” Kate said. “Nobody does.”

Jim leaned back in his chair.

“She's bitter because I won.”

“Lesbianism is not a competition.”

Pilar looked at the flannel, the boots, the carabiner.

Then at Kate.

Her eyes warmed with something Jim couldn't quite name.

“I'm staying out of this.”

“Coward,” Jim said.

Pilar laughed softly.

Kate's eyes dropped to Jim again.

God, the shirt.

He caught her.

Again.

“Seriously?”

“Menu.”

“Your menu is down there.”

Kate looked down.

It was.

Pilar's eyebrows lifted.

Kate picked it up with dignity.

“Wine,” she said.

“Excellent idea,” Pilar replied.

Jim grinned.

Kate kicked him lightly under the table.

His boot touched hers.

Neither moved away immediately.

Pilar gave them recommendations without rushing them. When she left, Jim watched her cross the room.

Then something else caught his attention.

A butch woman passing their table gave him a quick smile and shifted sideways to make room between the chairs. Her eyes touched the carabiner, then his face, with the easy recognition of someone registering a familiar bit of visual language.

No pause.

No question.

No correction.

Jim looked after her.

Kate watched him.

His shoulders stayed down.

“Huh,” he said.

“What?”

“Nothing.”

Kate smiled into her wine list.

Dinner began slowly.

That was apparently how Amour Noir did things.

Bread arrived. Wine arrived. Nobody appeared to be counting the minutes between them. The room remained alive around Jim and Kate: low conversation, soft laughter, silverware against china, Selene receiving another couple at the front, Simone Laurent moving through the dining room in a silk blouse and sweeping skirt with the calm precision of someone who seemed to know what a table needed before the table did.

Jim noticed that nobody hovered.

Nobody tried to sell them speed.

Nobody made them feel like their chairs belonged to the next reservation.

He liked that.

He liked a lot of things tonight.

That was becoming suspicious.

Kate rested her chin briefly against her knuckles and watched him look around.

Jim caught her.

“Again.”

“What?”

“You're staring.”

“I'm allowed.”

“Why?”

“Free country.”

“That's not a reason.”

Kate's eyes moved over him once more.

Jim waited for the joke.

It didn't come.

“What?” he asked again.

Kate considered lying.

Then decided she had already suffered enough.

“You look good.”

Jim's expression changed.

The swagger vanished for half a second.

“What?”

“The flannel.”

“Oh.”

“And the boots.”

He looked down.

“And, God help me, the stupid carabiner.”

Jim's eyes came back up.

Kate took a drink.

“You're enjoying this.”

“I am not.”

“You are.”

“I resent it.”

“Why?”

“Because you have been doing this for approximately forty minutes.”

“Some people are gifted.”

“There. That.”

“What?”

“That fucking face.”

Jim smiled wider.

Kate pointed at him.

“You are way too pleased with yourself.”

“I conquered lesbianism.”

“You put on a shirt.”

“And boots.”

“And a carabiner.”

“Thank you.”

“That was not support.”

Jim leaned back, comfortable, one ankle crossing over the other.

Kate's eyes followed the movement.

He caught her.

She didn't even bother denying it this time.

“Oh,” Jim said.

“Shut up.”

“You think I'm hot.”

Kate stared at him.

Jim's confidence wavered.

Just enough.

Kate could have saved him.

She chose violence.

“Yes.”

Jim went completely still.

Kate felt viciously satisfied for almost two seconds.

Then Jim's cheeks colored.

That was worse.

Much worse.

“Don't do that,” Kate said.

“Do what?”

“That.”

“I'm sitting.”

“Blush.”

Jim touched his face.

Kate laughed.

“Fuck you.”

“No. You started this.”

“I asked a question.”

“You made an accusation.”

“And you confessed.”

“I answered honestly.”

“That seems irresponsible.”

Kate leaned toward him.

“Jim.”

“What?”

“You are abso-fucking-lutely adorable like this.”

His eyes widened.

“And hot,” she added.

“Kate.”

“What?”

“You can't just—”

“Apparently I can.”

He looked down at the table.

Kate saw the old machinery twitch.

The impulse to minimize. Correct. Explain.

Then a server passed behind him and said something quietly to Pilar at the service station. Jim glanced over. The moment moved on.

His shoulders never rose.

The wall never quite made it into place.

When he looked back, he was smiling.

Smaller.

Real.

Kate's chest hurt.

“That's concerning,” he said.

“What is?”

“You finding me hot.”

“Deeply.”

“Could be a medical condition.”

“We'll monitor it.”

Jim laughed.

Across the room, a woman caught the sound and smiled reflexively before returning to her table.

Jim saw that too.

He looked around.

Once.

Twice.

Kate could practically see him counting without knowing he was counting.

Pilar returned with their first course.

She set Kate's plate down, then Jim's.

“Anything else?”

“No,” Jim said. “This looks amazing.”

Pilar smiled.

It arrived, but a fraction late.

Jim noticed.

Kate saw him notice.

Pilar moved away before either could say more.

Kate looked at Jim.

He was watching Pilar.

“What?”

“Nothing.”

“Your nothing is creepy.”

“You like my creepy.”

Kate smiled despite herself.

Dinner moved on.

So did Pilar.

At first Kate thought Jim had imagined it.

Pilar was too good at her job for the room to show much. She never rushed a table. Never snapped. Never dropped the calm softness from her voice.

But once Kate started watching, she saw the seams.

Pilar paused at the service station with both hands braced against the edge for one breath too long.

A moment later she crossed the room perfectly composed.

She smiled at a table, turned away, and the smile vanished before she'd completed the turn.

When Greta Sorensen passed with a reset, Pilar thanked her and touched her arm briefly, like the contact had steadied something.

Nothing dramatic.

Just a woman having a shit night while being extremely competent in public.

Not for long.

Just enough.

Kate knew he was tracking her.

The realization did something warm and dangerous inside her.

“How is everything?”

“Excellent,” Kate said.

“Good.”

Kate knew that hesitation.

Usually it was where he checked himself.

How am I standing?

How does this sound?

What are they seeing?

Should I make a joke?

Should I make this more masculine, less soft, less dangerous?

For one second, the restaurant disappeared from her face.

Not because she broke down.

Because she looked tired.

“Okay.”

No challenge.

No insistence.

“If you forget something, we don't care. If something takes forever, we don't care. If you need five minutes and our water glasses become tragically empty, we'll write our memoirs about surviving it.”

At the front, Selene caught Jim's eye when he looked toward the door and gave him a tiny, knowing smile. Jim glanced behind himself as if it might have been intended for someone else.

It wasn't.

Later, a butch diner returning to her table noticed the carabiner when Jim stood to let her pass between chairs. Her eyes flicked to it, then to his boots, then she gave him the smallest approving nod.

Jim sat down slowly.

Kate saw his face.

He looked around the room.

Not anxiously now.

Testing.

A couple at the bar-height wine ledge glanced toward their table when Kate laughed. One woman smiled at Jim; the other looked at Kate and smiled too.

No confusion.

No recalculation.

No silent demand that he explain what category he belonged in before they could proceed.

Just people.

Just a Friday night.

Just St. Claire.

Jim's brow furrowed.

Kate waited.

He looked down at his shirt.

Then his boots.

Then the carabiner.

His face changed.

Not revelation.

Satisfaction.

“Oh my God.”

Kate nearly laughed.

“What?”

“I figured it out.”

Her heart kicked.

“Figured what out?”

Jim leaned toward her.

“I think I'm finally getting good at being a guy.”

Kate stared at him.

There were moments in life when a person was called upon to demonstrate character.

This was apparently hers.

She did not laugh.

It nearly killed her.

Jim gestured at himself.

“Seriously. Think about it.”

Kate bit the inside of her cheek.

“I'm thinking.”

“I've spent my entire life doing it wrong.”

“Doing what wrong?”

“This.”

He indicated the whole ridiculous package.

“Clothes. The way I carry myself. All of it.”

Kate looked at the flannel.

The boots.

The carabiner.

His narrow little body relaxed into the chair.

The soft happiness in his face.

The complete absence of the wall.

She loved him a little for being so spectacularly, beautifully wrong about the reason.

She also wasn't entirely sure he was wrong about the result.

Maybe that was the point.

Maybe this really was somewhere Jim could live.

Maybe it was a doorway.

Maybe it was both.

Kate didn't know.

And for once, she didn't need to.

Jim was still talking.

“People aren't doing the thing.”

Kate's amusement vanished.

“What thing?”

“You know.”

She did.

“The look.”

Jim nodded.

“Usually there's this second where people decide they got something wrong.”

Kate stayed quiet.

“And then I have to—”

He stopped.

His shoulders shifted.

There it was in miniature.

The beginning of the explanation.

Kate watched him notice it.

Then let it go.

He looked around the room again.

“Nobody here cares.”

Kate's chest tightened.

“No,” she said softly.

Jim smiled.

Not triumphant.

Relieved.

Then the swagger came back.

“So obviously I've mastered masculinity.”

Kate choked on her wine.

Jim grinned.

“There it is.”

“You are unbelievable.”

“Admit it.”

“I admit nothing.”

“You already admitted I'm hot.”

“That was before you became insufferable.”

“So twenty minutes ago.”

“Longest twenty minutes of my life.”

Jim leaned back.

The carabiner clicked.

Kate looked at it.

Then at him.

He caught her.

“Still hot?”

Kate sighed.

“Unfortunately.”

His smile turned almost shy.

The combination was obscene.

Kate reached for her wine.

“This is bullshit.”

“What?”

“You.”

“Specific.”

“You stumble into flannel and boots one time and suddenly you're walking around like you've been doing this for years.”

“Natural talent.”

“Stop saying that.”

“Jealous?”

“Deeply.”

Jim laughed.

Kate let herself look.

Really look.

Small. Pretty. Masculine and feminine cues refusing to cancel each other out. Comfortable enough that the contradiction stopped being a contradiction.

Futch, her brain supplied.

Maybe.

She did not say it.

It wasn't hers to hand him like a diagnosis.

But, Jesus Christ, it looked good on him.

More importantly, he looked good inside it.

Kate thought about the heels he'd stared at.

The softness that had crossed his face.

*There she is again.*

Maybe.

Maybe not.

Maybe Kate was seeing a doorway and getting ahead of the person walking through it.

So she did the only decent thing.

She stayed where Jim was.

And where Jim was, apparently, was conquering being a guy while looking like the hottest accidental futch chaos goblin Kate had ever seen.

Fine.

She could work with that.

Dessert became unavoidable.

Not because either wanted dessert.

Because ordering it allowed them to remain at the table without acknowledging that they were remaining at the table.

Jim understood this immediately.

“We should get dessert.”

Kate nodded.

“Platonically.”

“Obviously.”

“Medical necessity.”

“Blood sugar.”

“Exactly.”

Pilar heard the last part as she approached.

“Medical necessity?”

Kate closed her eyes.

Jim brightened.

“It's been a very health-conscious evening.”

Pilar looked at the two phones now sitting beside their respective glasses.

Then at Kate.

Then at Jim.

Kate said, “Don't.”

Pilar's mouth twitched.

“I didn't say anything.”

Jim pointed at Kate.

“See? Everybody does that to you.”

“I'm surrounded by terrible people.”

Pilar's smile was fully real now.

Not fixed.

Not magically cured.

Real.

“Dessert?”

“Yes,” Kate said. “Before he gets worse.”

“No promises,” Jim said.

Pilar took the order.

As she turned away, Jim said, “Pilar?”

She looked back.

He didn't ask if she was okay.

He just smiled.

She understood.

“Seven percent,” she said.

Jim nodded.

“Excellent progress.”

Pilar walked away.

Kate looked at him.

Jim looked back.

“What?”

She almost said it.

*You're beautiful.*

Not pretty.

Not hot.

Not adorable.

Beautiful in the way people were beautiful when you caught them being themselves.

Too much.

Way too much.

So Kate said, “You're annoying.”

Jim smiled.

“Still gave me your number.”

“For medical emergencies.”

“Right.”

Dessert came.

They shared it.

This was, according to both of them, not evidence.

When the check finally arrived, neither reached for it immediately.

Not because of money.

Because the check meant the night had acquired an edge.

There would be an after.

Kate had his number now.

That helped.

More than she wanted to admit.

Jim looked at the check.

Then at Kate.

“So.”

“So.”

“This was not a date.”

“Absolutely not.”

“Even though the restaurant thinks we're getting married.”

“Administrative error.”

“Even though you called me hot.”

“Temporary impairment.”

“Even though we exchanged numbers.”

“Medical preparedness.”

“Shared dessert?”

“Portion control.”

Jim nodded.

“Good.”

“Good.”

They stood.

Kate picked up the flamingo lamp.

Jim reached for it.

She moved it away.

“Mine.”

“You hate it.”

“I carried it through dinner.”

“That's not ownership.”

“It is under maritime law.”

“We're not at sea.”

“Prove it.”

Jim looked around.

“Concerningly difficult in this lighting.”

They made their way toward the front.

The room had thinned slightly but had not hurried. Selene remained at the front table, auburn hair catching the low light as she looked up.

Jim stopped.

Kate knew immediately that this was a mistake.

“For the record,” he said, “there was no proposal.”

Selene regarded him.

Then Kate.

Then the flamingo lamp.

“I'm sure you're relieved.”

“I am.”

“Good.”

Jim nodded.

Selene's expression stayed serene.

“Good luck with the medical emergency.”

Kate made a sound somewhere between a cough and a scream.

Jim turned.

“You told her?”

“I did not!”

Behind them, Pilar laughed.

Jim looked toward her.

Pilar had one hand over her mouth.

“You people communicate.”

Selene opened the door.

“We're a high-touch establishment.”

Kate doubled over laughing.

Jim pointed at Selene.

“That is abuse of hospitality language.”

“Good night, Jim.”

He stepped through the door.

Kate followed with the lamp.

Outside, she was still laughing.

Jim stood with his hands on his hips.

“This place is hostile.”

“No, it isn't.”

“It is.”

“You loved it.”

“I tolerated it.”

“You told Pilar you loved it.”

“That was before the conspiracy.”

Kate straightened.

Jim was smiling.

She looked at him under the Friday-night lights.

Flannel.

Boots.

Carabiner.

That face.

Relaxed shoulders.

No wall.

Her stomach did the stupid thing again.

Jim noticed her looking.

This time he didn't ask what.

He smiled.

Cocky little bastard.

Kate stepped closer.

Not enough to touch.

Enough that he noticed.

“Don't get smug.”

“About?”

“You know.”

“Being hot?”

Kate stared.

Jim's grin widened.

“Oh, you're going to be unbearable now.”

“Natural talent.”

“I hate you.”

“No, you don't.”

Kate looked at him.

At the person she'd watched with Pilar.

At the person who had spent the evening slowly discovering that a room full of strangers could see him without demanding an explanation.

At the ridiculous little man who thought he'd finally cracked masculinity.

At the softness he kept showing her when he forgot to hide it.

“No,” she said.

Jim's grin softened.

“Apparently not.”

They started walking.

Kate carried the flamingo lamp.

Jim's carabiner clicked softly at his hip.

After half a block, Jim pulled out his phone.

Kate's phone buzzed.

She stopped.

Jim kept walking.

She looked down.

A message from JIM CALDWELL.

**MEDICAL EMERGENCY.**

Kate looked up.

Jim was ten feet ahead, walking backward now.

She typed.

**Are you bleeding?**

His phone buzzed.

He read it.

Typed.

**Worse.**

Kate waited.

**I just remembered the ceramic cat.**

Kate laughed so loudly a woman across the street looked over.

Jim smiled.

Kate typed.

**Blocked.**

His answer came immediately.

**you'd miss me**

Kate's thumb stopped.

The joke was right there.

Easy.

Safe.

She looked up.

Jim had stopped walking.

He was watching her.

No swagger now.

Just waiting.

Kate typed.

**Unfortunately.**

Jim read it.

His face went soft.

There she was again.

Or there he was.

Or maybe, Kate thought, that was exactly the wrong question.

There you are.

Jim looked up from his phone.

Kate lifted the flamingo lamp.

“Still not a date!”

Jim laughed.

“Extremely not!”

And they kept walking.
