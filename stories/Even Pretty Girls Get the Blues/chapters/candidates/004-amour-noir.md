# Chapter Four — Candidate

Jim had been hungry for at least an hour.

This was not, in his opinion, relevant to the fact that he had followed Kate into a restaurant called Amour Noir.

“It means Black Love,” Kate said.

“I know enough French to be offended that you thought I needed that translated.”

Kate shifted the pink flamingo lamp under her arm. “You asked what kind of place it was.”

“I asked why you made that face.”

“I did not make a face.”

“You made a face.”

“I have a very expressive face.”

“You looked at the sign, looked at me, and went, ‘Oh.’”

“That is not a face.”

“That is a face with a soundtrack.”

Kate stopped beneath the restaurant’s name and looked at him.

Jim stopped too.

The carabiner still clipped to his belt gave a tiny metallic click.

Kate’s eyes dropped to it.

Jim caught her.

“Don’t.”

“I didn’t say anything.”

“You were about to.”

“I was admiring your credentials.”

“I earned these.”

“Twenty-seven minutes ago.”

“Seniority is seniority.”

Kate looked back at the entrance.

Amour Noir was open, warm candlelight visible beyond the glass, and very obviously not the kind of place where two people wandered in because they happened to be hungry and one of them happened to be carrying a thrift-store flamingo lamp.

Jim looked at the lamp.

Then at Kate.

“Maybe we should leave that outside.”

Kate’s head turned slowly.

“You bought it.”

“That is not an argument for bringing it to dinner.”

“I have carried this abomination through half of St. Claire.”

“You volunteered.”

“I took it away from you because I was afraid you’d buy it a friend.”

“There was a ceramic cat.”

“I know.”

“It wore a crown.”

“I know.”

“It could have held—”

“If you say emergency lesbian credentials, I am leaving you on the sidewalk.”

Jim smiled.

Kate opened the door.

“After you, provisional lesbian.”

“Chivalry is alive.”

“No. I need both hands for your lamp.”

“Still my lamp when it’s inconvenient, I notice.”

“Move.”

Jim went in.

The room was smaller than he expected.

Not cramped. Intimate.

Twenty-six seats, maybe, arranged for people who had come specifically to pay attention to whoever sat across from them. Candlelight softened the room without making it dark, and conversation stayed low enough that nobody had to compete with anybody else’s evening. Wine glasses caught little pieces of light. Nobody appeared to be in a hurry.

Jim took in the room.

Then the couples.

Then the room again.

“Oh.”

Kate came in behind him.

Jim looked at her.

Kate looked innocent.

“You knew.”

“I knew what?”

“This is a date restaurant.”

Kate’s mouth twitched.

“It is a restaurant.”

“There are levels of restaurant.”

“Are there?”

“Yes.”

“Explain them.”

“Diner.”

“Good start.”

“Normal restaurant.”

“Very rigorous taxonomy.”

“Fancy restaurant.”

“And?”

Jim gestured around them.

“This.”

Kate looked around as though seeing it for the first time.

“I don’t understand.”

“Bullshit.”

A woman at the front table looked up.

She was a white woman in her early forties, average height, with long, wavy auburn hair and the composed, unhurried air of someone who could probably end an argument simply by waiting for everybody else to become embarrassed. Her silk dress made Jim suddenly aware that he was standing in a romantic dinner club with a carabiner clipped to his belt and a pink flamingo lamp peering over Kate’s arm.

“Good evening,” she said.

Her voice had the same quiet authority as the rest of her.

Kate smiled. “Hi.”

“Do you have a reservation?”

Jim and Kate looked at each other.

“No,” they said together.

The woman’s expression did not change.

Jim said, “This was spontaneous.”

Kate added, “Extremely spontaneous.”

“We were hungry.”

“Famished.”

“This is not—”

Kate turned her head.

Jim stopped.

The woman waited.

Jim could feel the trap.

He stepped into it anyway.

“This isn’t a date.”

Kate closed her eyes.

The woman at the front table looked at Jim.

Then at Kate.

Then, very briefly, at the flamingo lamp.

“Of course.”

Jim relaxed.

Kate did not.

The woman checked the room, then the reservation book in front of her.

“We do have one two-top available. I can seat you if you don’t mind that we won’t rush the table.”

Jim blinked.

“You’re warning us that we can stay?”

“We find it prevents misunderstandings.”

“That may be the best restaurant policy I’ve ever heard.”

The woman’s mouth softened by approximately one millimeter.

Kate said, “We promise not to make trouble.”

Jim looked at her.

Kate looked back.

The woman picked up two menus.

“Good luck with your proposal.”

Jim stared.

Kate made a small choking sound.

“Our what?”

But the woman was already moving.

Kate caught Jim by the sleeve.

“Do not.”

“What proposal?”

“Jim.”

“I specifically said not a date.”

“Yes.”

“So where the hell did proposal come from?”

Kate’s shoulders started shaking.

Jim stared at her.

“Oh, fuck you.”

“I didn’t say anything.”

“You’re laughing.”

“I’m not.”

“You are visibly vibrating.”

The woman glanced back.

Kate immediately composed herself.

Jim leaned closer.

“This is your fault.”

“My fault?”

“You picked the restaurant.”

“We were walking.”

“You knew what it was.”

“I knew they served food.”

“You knew they served proposals.”

Kate lost the fight with her mouth.

“Apparently.”

Jim pointed at her.

“This is not funny.”

“It is a little funny.”

“It is not.”

The woman stopped beside the available table.

Kate whispered, “Good luck with your proposal.”

Jim nearly walked into a chair.

The woman either did not notice or possessed the professionalism to pretend she had not.

Jim was beginning to respect her deeply.

The table was small enough that Jim and Kate would not be able to pretend distance existed between them. Candlelight sat between the place settings. The rest of the dining room continued around them in low voices, glass against glass, the soft movement of service that never seemed to break anybody’s conversation.

Kate carefully set the flamingo lamp beside her chair.

Jim sat.

Then looked at it.

The flamingo looked back.

“This feels judgmental.”

“It knows what you did.”

“I bought it for charity.”

“You bought it because it was ugly.”

“Two things can be true.”

Kate sat opposite him.

The candlelight caught in her hair.

Jim looked.

He had been looking at Kate all night, but Amour Noir was apparently designed by people who considered plausible deniability a moral failing.

Her hair looked ridiculous.

Not ridiculous bad.

Ridiculous as in nobody should be allowed to have that much of it and also know how to use eye contact.

Kate noticed.

“What?”

Jim picked up the menu.

“Nothing.”

She smiled.

“Your nothing is getting less convincing.”

“I’m reading.”

“You’re holding the menu upside down.”

Jim looked down.

It was not upside down.

He looked up.

Kate was grinning.

“You’re an asshole.”

“You checked.”

“Because you said—”

“You checked.”

Jim set the menu down.

“I liked you better before dinner.”

“No, you didn’t.”

The awful part was that she was right.

A server approached their table.

Jim noticed her first because she moved differently from the room.

Not badly.

Almost the opposite.

She was a Colombian Latina in her mid-thirties, average height, poised, dark hair secured in a low chignon, her fitted black dress clean and deliberate, her pace unhurried even with a full Friday room moving around her. She looked like someone who knew exactly how long a table needed before interruption.

But when she reached them, the smile arrived a fraction after the rest of her.

Tiny.

Probably invisible if Jim hadn’t spent years studying the half second between what people felt and what they decided to show him.

“Good evening. I’m Pilar.”

Her voice was steady.

Kate smiled. “Kate.”

“Jim.”

Pilar’s eyes flicked between them with no visible confusion, correction, or recalculation.

Jim waited for it anyway.

Nothing.

Her attention moved naturally to the table.

“We pace dinner around you here. If you want time, take it. If you want recommendations, I’m happy to help. And if you’d rather sit with the wine list and ignore me for ten minutes, I promise not to be offended.”

Jim smiled.

“I already like this place.”

“I’ll try not to ruin it.”

The words were light.

The expression behind them wasn’t.

Then Pilar was professional again.

Kate had noticed too. Jim could tell because she did not make a joke.

They ordered drinks and let Pilar go.

Jim watched her cross the room.

Kate watched Jim.

“What?”

“Nothing.”

“Bullshit.”

“You taught me that word.”

“I did not invent bullshit.”

“You’ve certainly refined it.”

Jim looked back at the menu.

Kate let him.

Dinner began to unfold rather than arrive.

That was the first thing Jim noticed about Amour Noir. Nobody seemed to be trying to get rid of them. A plate could sit empty for a minute without someone swooping in as though porcelain at rest were a crisis. Conversations around them stretched. Wine was discussed instead of sold. Somewhere behind Jim, a woman laughed quietly and then immediately lowered her voice, not because anybody had told her to, but because the room made privacy feel reciprocal.

It should have made Jim relax.

Mostly, it did.

Except for the part where he was sitting across from Kate.

At Amour Noir.

Not dating.

Under candlelight.

With a flamingo lamp.

Kate took a sip of wine.

Jim said, “This proves nothing.”

She put the glass down.

“I didn’t say anything.”

“You were thinking.”

“I do that occasionally.”

“You were thinking date.”

“I was thinking wine.”

“Liar.”

“Prove it.”

Jim leaned back.

Kate’s eyes followed him.

“See?”

“See what?”

“That.”

“What?”

“You looked.”

“At you?”

“Yes.”

“You are sitting directly in front of me.”

“Convenient.”

“For looking at you?”

Jim opened his mouth.

Kate smiled.

He closed it.

“Five to zero,” she said.

“That scoreboard was invalidated.”

“By whom?”

“The governing body.”

“You are the governing body.”

“Exactly.”

Kate laughed.

Pilar returned with the next course.

The smile was there on time now.

Almost.

She placed Kate’s plate, then Jim’s.

“Anything else?”

Jim looked at her.

There it was again.

Not tears.

Not disaster.

Just effort.

The kind that became visible only when somebody was very good at hiding it.

“No,” Jim said. “We’re good.”

Pilar nodded.

Then Jim added, “And we are in absolutely no hurry.”

Something in her face changed.

Barely.

“I’m glad.”

“I mean it. If we’re your easiest table tonight, I consider that a personal achievement.”

Kate looked at him.

Pilar’s smile became real for the first time.

“Dangerous thing to say in a restaurant.”

“I’m living dangerously. I bought a lamp.”

Pilar looked down.

The flamingo lamp sat beside Kate’s chair in all its thrift-store glory.

Pilar stared at it.

Then back at Jim.

“I have questions.”

“Everyone does.”

Kate said, “Do not encourage him.”

Pilar laughed.

It escaped her before she could stop it.

Not loud.

But real.

Jim smiled.

“There. Easiest table.”

“You’re making a strong case.”

She moved away.

Kate was still looking at him.

“What?”

“Nothing.”

“Stop using my word.”

“Eat your dinner.”

Jim narrowed his eyes.

Kate took another sip of wine.

Dinner continued.

So did Pilar’s night.

Jim could see it happening in fragments.

A pause at the front table with the auburn-haired woman.

A hand briefly pressed flat against the edge of a service station before Pilar straightened and returned to the room.

A moment when she stopped, closed her eyes for one second, then opened them and moved again.

No catastrophe.

No single thing Jim could point to.

Just a person using every bit of her professionalism to keep a bad night from becoming everybody else’s problem.

Jim knew that trick.

Eventually Kate excused herself.

“Where are you going?”

Kate stared at him.

“Do you require a written itinerary?”

“We’re not dating. I don’t know the rules.”

“The restroom, you lunatic.”

“Thank you for maintaining transparency.”

“Guard the lamp.”

“No promises.”

“If you buy it another lamp while I’m gone, I will kill you.”

Jim watched her walk away.

Then immediately became aware that he had watched her walk away.

“Quality control,” he muttered.

“Excuse me?”

Jim looked up.

Pilar stood beside the table.

“Oh. Nothing.”

Her eyebrow lifted.

Jim pointed at the empty chair.

“Long story.”

“I work in a romantic restaurant.”

“Not romantic.”

“Of course.”

Jim stared.

Pilar’s mouth twitched.

“Did Selene get to you?”

“The woman at the front?”

“Long auburn hair?”

“Yes.”

“Then yes.”

“She wished us luck with our proposal.”

Pilar looked toward the front table.

For one second, the terrible night lost.

She laughed.

Actually laughed.

“Oh no.”

“What?”

“Nothing.”

“You people are evil.”

“We’re very caring.”

“That’s worse.”

Pilar reached for his empty plate.

Her hand slipped.

Not enough to drop it.

Enough that the edge clicked sharply against the table.

She froze.

Jim did too.

Pilar recovered immediately.

“Sorry.”

“You didn’t break anything.”

“I know.”

But she said it too quickly.

Jim looked at her.

Pilar looked away.

And there it was.

The wall.

Different wall.

Same architecture.

Jim lowered his voice.

“Hey.”

She looked back.

“I’m going to say something incredibly selfish.”

That surprised her enough to interrupt whatever apology she had been preparing.

“Okay.”

“I need you to do me a favor.”

Pilar waited.

“Stop trying to be perfect at this table.”

Her face went still.

Jim continued before he could lose his nerve.

“I don’t know what kind of night you’re having. I’m not asking. But you don’t have to spend energy pretending it isn’t happening for us.”

Pilar’s fingers remained around the plate.

Jim nodded toward Kate’s empty chair.

“She’s terrifying, but mostly to me.”

That got the smallest breath of laughter.

“And I’m wearing a carabiner I bought less than an hour ago because I apparently joined a union.”

Pilar looked at it.

Jim looked down too.

“Longer story.”

“I assumed.”

“So if you need five minutes, take five minutes. If you forget something, we’ll survive. If dinner takes forever, apparently that’s your entire business model.”

Pilar stared at him.

Jim suddenly felt like an idiot.

He almost retreated.

Almost said he was kidding.

Almost built the wall for her.

Instead he left the words where they were.

Pilar’s shoulders lowered.

Not much.

Enough.

“You do this often?” she asked.

“What?”

“Tell strangers they can stop performing.”

Jim blinked.

“No.”

“Maybe you should.”

He laughed once.

“That sounds exhausting.”

Pilar looked at him for another moment.

“Thank you.”

Jim shrugged because he had no idea what else to do with his body.

“Selfish. Remember? I’m trying to win easiest table.”

“You’re cheating.”

“That accusation has followed me all night.”

“I believe it.”

She took the plate.

Then stopped.

“I am going to take five minutes.”

“Excellent.”

“And if anybody asks, you did not give me permission.”

“I would never undermine the chain of command.”

“Good.”

Pilar walked away.

Jim looked down at his hands.

The candle between the place settings flickered.

He became intensely interested in it.

When Kate returned, she slowed before reaching the table.

Jim did not see why.

Kate did.

Across the room, Pilar stood for a moment near the front table with Selene. Whatever passed between them was quiet. Selene touched Pilar’s forearm once. Pilar said something. Selene nodded toward the back.

Pilar disappeared from the floor.

Jim was sitting alone at their table, staring suspiciously hard at a candle.

Kate looked from the place where Pilar had disappeared to Jim.

Then back.

She sat down.

“What did you do?”

Jim looked up too quickly.

“Nothing.”

“Jim.”

“Why does everyone keep accusing me of things?”

“Because you have a face.”

“So do you.”

“Mine is beautiful and innocent.”

“Half right.”

Kate stared at him.

Jim reached for his wine.

Kate kept staring.

“What?”

“You did something.”

“I sat here.”

“Pilar just left the floor.”

“People are allowed to move.”

“Selene sent her toward the back.”

“Maybe there’s cake.”

Kate narrowed her eyes.

Jim drank.

“Fine,” Kate said.

“Fine.”

“I won’t ask.”

“Great.”

“I’ll simply infer.”

“That seems unethical.”

“I’m comfortable with it.”

Pilar came back several minutes later.

Nothing miraculous had happened.

Her night had not been repaired.

Whatever had been waiting for her before those five minutes was presumably still waiting.

But the effort had changed.

The smile reached her eyes this time.

She stopped at their table.

“Still alive?”

Jim checked himself.

“Apparently.”

Kate said, “He’s difficult to kill.”

“I’m learning.”

Pilar looked at Jim.

“Thank you for your patience.”

Jim’s expression shifted.

Just a little.

Kate saw it.

“No problem.”

Pilar moved on.

Kate watched her go.

Then she looked at Jim.

He was pretending to study the wine list.

*God damn it.*

Kate picked up her glass.

*I did not just see that.*

Jim looked over the top of the list.

“What?”

“Nothing.”

“You’re staring at me.”

“No, I’m not.”

“You absolutely are.”

Kate drank.

Jim waited.

She set the glass down.

“You’re annoying.”

“That seems unrelated.”

“It isn’t.”

“What did I do?”

“Exist aggressively.”

Jim smiled.

Kate hated that too.

The dinner went on.

Food.

Wine.

Arguments about whether Jim’s provisional lesbian status entitled him to voting rights.

A serious dispute over whether the flamingo lamp was kitsch, camp, or evidence.

Kate claimed evidence.

Jim demanded an attorney.

Somewhere in the middle of it, the room stopped feeling like a trap and started feeling like theirs for the evening.

That was worse.

Much worse.

By the time the last plates were gone, Jim had stopped checking the room every time somebody looked toward him.

Kate noticed that too.

A woman passed their table on her way out, glanced at Jim’s carabiner, then at Kate, and smiled the tiny private smile of somebody who had apparently understood something.

Jim saw it.

Kate saw him see it.

His body waited.

Kate knew the sequence now.

The shoulders.

The correction.

The wall.

It didn’t come.

Jim looked briefly confused.

Then the woman was gone.

He looked down at the carabiner.

“Huh.”

Kate’s heart did something deeply unhelpful.

“What?”

“Nothing.”

“Jim.”

He looked up.

“I think I’m getting away with it.”

“With what?”

He gestured vaguely at himself.

Kate understood exactly what he meant.

She also understood that he didn’t.

Her instinct was to touch him.

She didn’t.

Instead she smiled.

“Natural talent.”

Jim’s face lit.

“Exactly.”

Kate looked away before she did something catastrophic, like fall in love with him in a restaurant designed for exactly that purpose.

Selene passed their table.

Kate caught her eye.

Selene looked at Jim.

Then Kate.

Her gaze dropped briefly to the flamingo lamp.

“Going well?”

Jim said, “No proposals.”

Selene’s expression remained serenely professional.

“Evening’s young.”

Kate covered her mouth.

Jim pointed after Selene as she walked away.

“She is a menace.”

Kate laughed into her hand.

“You walked into that.”

“I walked into a restaurant.”

“Same thing.”

Eventually there was nothing left to order without admitting they were staying simply because neither wanted to leave.

That realization sat between them.

Jim looked at the candle.

Kate looked at Jim.

Jim looked up.

“We should probably go.”

“Probably.”

Neither moved.

Jim frowned.

“There’s a logistical problem.”

“The lamp?”

“Other than the lamp.”

“The carabiner?”

“That is infrastructure.”

“Of course.”

Jim picked up his phone from beside his plate.

Kate looked at it.

Then at him.

“What?”

Jim turned the phone once in his hand.

“We don’t have each other’s numbers.”

Kate blinked.

They had smoked a cigar badly together.

Walked together through St. Claire.

Bought each other clothes.

Discussed gender in ways Kate had not discussed gender with people she had known for years.

Flirted until plausible deniability required industrial equipment.

Eaten dinner at Amour Noir.

And they did not have each other’s phone numbers.

Kate started laughing.

Jim frowned.

“It’s not that funny.”

“It’s incredibly funny.”

“I assumed we did.”

“How?”

“I don’t know.”

“You thought proximity had Bluetooth?”

“Maybe St. Claire has municipal lesbian networking.”

Kate laughed harder.

Jim waited.

Eventually she got herself under control.

Then she looked at him.

And there it was.

The stupidly simple sentence.

*Give me your number.*

Too direct.

Too obvious.

Too date.

Kate could feel the entire evening watching her.

So naturally she panicked.

“Give me your number.”

Jim’s eyebrows rose.

Kate immediately regretted having a mouth.

“That sounded—”

“Date-like?”

“No.”

“Yes.”

“No.”

“Kate.”

“I need it.”

Jim leaned back.

“For?”

Kate’s brain emptied.

Every reasonable answer fled.

She found the only one left.

“Medical emergency.”

Silence.

Jim stared at her.

Kate held his gaze with the dignity of a woman who had just driven a conversational car through a plate-glass window and intended to claim the building had moved.

“Medical emergency,” Jim repeated.

“Yes.”

“What medical emergency?”

“I don’t know. That’s why it’s an emergency.”

Jim’s mouth twitched.

Kate continued because stopping now would mean death.

“If something happens.”

“To whom?”

“You.”

“What?”

“Or me.”

“Kate.”

“Medical things happen.”

“They do.”

“Constantly.”

“Constantly?”

“People have bodies.”

Jim nodded solemnly.

“That’s true.”

“Bodies are unreliable.”

“Famously.”

“So.”

“So.”

“Your number.”

Jim looked at her for a long moment.

Kate refused to blink.

Then he unlocked his phone.

“Fine.”

“Fine?”

“For medical emergencies.”

“Exactly.”

“Nothing social.”

“God, no.”

“No texting because you found a shirt.”

“Absolutely not.”

“No pictures of terrible lamps.”

“I would block you.”

“No asking me to dinner.”

Kate hesitated.

Jim saw it.

His smile changed.

“Medical dinner?”

“Shut up.”

He handed her his phone.

Kate entered her number.

Then called herself.

Her phone buzzed on the table.

Jim looked at it.

Kate looked at him.

There it was.

A tiny stupid bridge.

One phone to the other.

Nothing dramatic.

Nothing anyone else in the restaurant would notice.

Kate saved the contact.

JIM CALDWELL.

She looked at the name.

Then at him.

Jim was doing the same with hers.

KATE RIVERA.

He smiled.

Kate’s chest tightened.

Jim said, “If you text me tonight, I’m assuming you’re dying.”

“Obviously.”

“If you send me a picture of something from the thrift store—”

“Stroke.”

“Shoes?”

“Severe neurological event.”

“Dress?”

“Organ failure.”

Jim nodded.

“Good to establish protocol.”

“Preparedness saves lives.”

Pilar arrived with the check.

She looked between them.

Then at the two phones.

Then at the flamingo lamp.

Her expression was perfectly professional.

“Everything all right?”

Kate said, “Medical emergency.”

Pilar looked at Jim.

Jim nodded gravely.

“Preventative.”

Pilar closed her eyes for one second.

When she opened them, she was smiling.

“I’m glad we could help.”

Jim handed over the check.

Pilar took it.

As she turned away, Kate said, “Pilar?”

She looked back.

Kate smiled.

“Have a better night.”

Pilar’s expression softened.

“I am.”

She looked at Jim for half a second.

Then she went.

Kate turned slowly toward him.

Jim became fascinated by the candle again.

“Don’t.”

“I didn’t say anything.”

“You’re about to.”

“I was going to say you’re a good person.”

Jim’s face changed immediately.

The humor disappeared.

Not defensively.

Almost worse.

He looked uncomfortable.

Kate understood.

So she let him off the hook.

“Which is very inconvenient for me.”

Jim looked up.

There she was.

The smile.

“You’ll survive.”

“Maybe.”

“Now you have my number in case you don’t.”

Kate laughed.

They finally stood.

Kate collected her bag and the flamingo lamp.

Jim reached for the lamp.

She moved it away.

“I’ve got it.”

“It’s mine.”

“You’ve demonstrated poor judgment.”

“I bought one lamp.”

“You considered the cat.”

“It had storage potential.”

“No.”

They crossed the room.

At the front, Selene looked up from her table.

Jim stopped.

Kate nearly walked into him.

He looked at Selene.

“For the record, there was no proposal.”

Selene regarded him.

Then Kate.

Then the flamingo lamp.

“I’m sure you’re very relieved.”

“I am.”

“Good.”

Jim nodded.

Kate could feel disaster approaching.

Selene smiled.

“Good luck with the medical emergency.”

Kate made a sound that was not human.

Jim turned on her.

“You told her?”

“I did not!”

Behind them, Pilar was laughing.

Jim looked from Pilar to Selene to Kate.

“This restaurant is a hostile environment.”

Selene opened the door for them.

“Good night.”

Kate walked through first because she could no longer be trusted to speak.

Jim followed.

Outside, the door closed behind them.

For three seconds, neither said anything.

Then Kate lost it.

She bent forward around the flamingo lamp, laughing so hard she had to brace one hand on her knee.

Jim stood beside her in offended silence.

“You done?”

“No.”

“I hate this place.”

“No, you don’t.”

“No.”

Kate looked up.

Jim was smiling.

Damn him.

“No,” he admitted.

Kate straightened.

They started walking.

The carabiner clicked softly against Jim’s belt.

Kate carried the flamingo lamp.

Their phones now carried each other.

Nothing else had been decided.

Jim glanced at her.

“So.”

“So?”

“If I experience a medical emergency involving a thrift store tomorrow—”

“Don’t.”

“What if it’s serious?”

“Jim.”

“What if there’s a sale?”

Kate looked at him.

He was trying very hard not to smile.

She pointed the flamingo lamp at him.

“Do not make me regret giving you my number.”

Jim’s smile won.

“Too late.”

Kate’s did too.

And they kept walking.
