# Chapter Seven — Friday

Kate woke up Friday morning with three facts.

First, she had slept badly.

Second, this was entirely Jim Caldwell's fault.

Third, the shoes were worth it.

She lay in bed for another thirty seconds staring at the ceiling before reaching for her phone.

There was already a message waiting.

**JIM:** Morning.

Then, sent four minutes later:

**JIM:** This is a purely administrative question.

Kate smiled despite herself.

**KATE:** That sentence has never once been followed by anything administrative.

The typing bubble appeared immediately.

**JIM:** Hypothetically, if a person owned six throw pillows, how many of them are allowed to be useless before it becomes a moral failure?

Kate sat up.

**KATE:** Six.

There was a pause.

**JIM:** Monster.

**KATE:** Pillows should be pretty.

**JIM:** Pillows should support the human body.

**KATE:** This is why men aren't allowed to decorate.

**JIM:** Sexist.

**KATE:** Accurate.

**JIM:** I have decorative pillows.

Kate narrowed her eyes at the screen.

**KATE:** You have decorative pillows that you have somehow optimized for lumbar support.

A longer pause.

**JIM:** I don't appreciate being known this well before coffee.

Kate laughed.

And there it was again.

That strange little domestic current that had developed between them almost without permission.

Flowers.

Fabric.

Shoes.

Food.

Furniture.

The correct weight of a towel.

Whether linen napkins were worth ironing.

Whether a room could contain too many lamps.

Jim had very strong opinions about lamps.

Kate had discovered this on Tuesday and had been exploiting it ever since.

She got out of bed.

**KATE:** Playing hooky still happening?

His answer came before she reached the bathroom.

**JIM:** I have already committed several acts of calendar fraud.

**KATE:** Proud of you.

**JIM:** You are a terrible influence.

**KATE:** Says the man who sent me home with tamales.

Kate stopped.

There it was.

The container was still in her refrigerator.

Tomorrow, he'd said.

Except tomorrow was now today.

Her smile softened.

Then her phone buzzed.

**JIM:** Eat them.

Kate stared at the message.

**KATE:** Are you in my apartment?

**JIM:** No.

**KATE:** Can you see me?

**JIM:** No.

**KATE:** Then how—

**JIM:** Because you're sentimental and were considering preserving them like archaeological evidence.

Kate looked toward the kitchen.

“Asshole.”

Her phone buzzed.

**JIM:** I heard that spiritually.

---

Jim changed his shirt twice.

Not because there was anything wrong with the first one.

Or the second one.

This was important.

There was nothing happening here.

He and Kate were playing hooky.

They were going secondhand shopping.

Then she was taking him somewhere she loved.

He had made lunch.

There was no reason whatsoever for any of this to require standing in front of his mirror for fifteen minutes adjusting a waistcoat by fractions of an inch.

The trousers were easy.

Very dark. High-waisted. Slim.

Women's, technically, but that had stopped registering as particularly significant somewhere around the point where he'd discovered they fit him better.

The white top was fitted enough to keep the waistcoat clean and close against his body. The charcoal-black waistcoat gave him exactly the line he wanted: narrow waist, sharp shoulders, tidy silhouette.

The boots worked.

The carabiner worked.

He looked—

Jim tilted his head.

Good.

Really good.

There was something satisfying about the whole thing. Something he had been trying to find for years without knowing he was looking for it.

Masculine, he decided.

But better.

Intentional.

He reached automatically toward the small tray on his dresser.

His fingers found the fine gold chain.

He had bought it because—

Well.

Because the outfit needed something.

That was all.

The chain settled delicately at his throat, barely there against the white.

Jim looked at himself again.

Finished.

“Yep.”

He grabbed his keys.

Mystery solved.

Masculinity conquered.

---

Kate had not conquered anything.

Kate had declared war.

The dress had been purchased for this exact purpose, although she would deny that under oath.

It followed her body without apology.

The lingerie underneath matched because of course it did. Nobody was going to see it. That was not the point.

Kate knew it was there.

Her hair was deliberate. Her makeup was deliberate. Her jewelry was deliberate. Her fragrance was deliberate.

And the Louboutins—

Kate looked down.

“Oh, absolutely.”

This was not dressing for Jim.

That would be ridiculous.

This was dressing because Jim had shown up in a waistcoat and somehow made Kate spend several days thinking thoughts that should have required a permit.

There were rules.

There were consequences.

She picked up her bag.

Her phone buzzed.

**JIM:** Here.

Kate looked toward the window.

Then back at herself.

“Let's see how masculine you feel now.”

---

Jim forgot what he was saying.

He had been saying something.

Possibly about parking.

Possibly about traffic.

Possibly about the continued existence of oxygen.

Kate stepped out of the building.

The bodycon dress hit him first.

Then the hair.

Then the heels.

His brain, which had previously performed software engineering for money, ceased all professional operations.

Kate approached the car.

Jim got out.

She stopped in front of him.

He looked at her.

She looked at him.

Her eyes dropped.

Trousers.

Waistcoat.

Carabiner.

Then the tiny glint of gold at his throat.

Something in her expression changed.

Not much.

Just enough.

Jim noticed.

“What?”

“Nothing.”

“That was absolutely something.”

Kate's eyes returned to his face.

“You accessorized.”

Jim touched the chain.

“This?”

“Yes, that.”

“It needed something.”

Kate's mouth twitched.

“Of course it did.”

Jim narrowed his eyes.

“Why are you saying it like that?”

“No reason.”

He looked down at her shoes.

Then slowly back up.

“Oh, come on.”

Kate smiled.

“What?”

“Those are hostile.”

“They're shoes.”

“Those are an escalation.”

“You wore a waistcoat.”

“That is clothing.”

“So is this.”

Jim stared at her.

Kate held out her arms.

“Are we going shopping or are you going to stand here being oppressed by footwear?”

“I need a minute.”

“Take two.”

Jim opened the passenger door for her.

Kate paused.

Not because he had opened it.

Because before she stepped in, he glanced at the narrow heel, then at the pavement, then shifted himself slightly so she had a clean angle into the car without having to negotiate the broken edge near the curb.

He didn't mention it.

Kate did not mention noticing.

She got in.

Jim closed the door.

Kate watched him walk around the front of the car.

“Oh, this is going to be a problem.”

---

The sign over the storefront read:

**MY EXES' ABANDONED STUFF**

Underneath, in smaller lettering:

**ASK ME ABOUT MY EXES**

Jim stood on the sidewalk.

Kate waited.

He read it again.

Then looked at her.

“No.”

“Yes.”

“That cannot be the name of a functioning business.”

“It functions beautifully.”

“Ask me about my exes?”

“That's the slogan.”

“Of course it is.”

Kate smiled.

“You wanted meaningful.”

“I wanted meaningful to you.”

“This is meaningful to me.”

Jim looked through the window at a carved cabinet, three lamps, a stack of framed art, what appeared to be a brass bird taller than Kate, and a ceramic object whose purpose had apparently died with its creator.

His face changed.

Kate saw it happen.

“Oh no.”

“What?”

“You like it already.”

“I have made no such statement.”

“You made the face.”

“What face?”

“The one where you're about to develop an opinion.”

Jim opened the door.

“I always have opinions.”

“That is exactly what I'm afraid of.”

A small bell sounded overhead.

The place smelled like old wood, clean fabric, paper, furniture wax, and time.

Not dust.

Time.

There was a difference.

The front of the shop refused to explain itself in any conventional retail language. A beautiful old sideboard held mismatched glassware. Lamps glowed from different heights. Art leaned against walls behind other art. A rack of textiles stood near shelves of ceramics. Books appeared wherever books had found enough horizontal surface to establish sovereignty.

Nothing matched.

Everything belonged.

Jim stopped three steps inside.

Kate watched him take it in.

“Oh,” he said.

That one syllable was almost reverent.

Kate's smile softened.

Then a woman's voice came from behind the counter.

“Rivera.”

Kate turned.

Mara Singh was standing with one hand resting on an open ledger.

Immaculate dress.

Immaculate nails.

Heels.

Hair perfect.

Her gaze went to Kate.

Then down.

She froze.

Her face lit with pure, delighted recognition.

“Ohhhhhhh.”

Kate closed her eyes.

Mara leaned slightly around the counter for a better view.

“The Loubis.”

“Mara.”

“No, no. Don't ‘Mara’ me.”

Jim looked between them.

Mara's gaze traveled from the shoes up the dress.

Then to Kate's face.

Then to Jim.

Then down Jim: fitted white top, waistcoat, narrow dark trousers, boots, carabiner.

Then the tiny gold chain.

Then back to Kate.

A beat.

Another.

Kate pointed toward the store.

“We're shopping for furniture.”

Mara's expression did not move.

“I didn't ask.”

Jim looked at Kate.

Kate looked straight ahead.

Mara smiled.

Not broadly.

That would have been cruel.

“Good morning.”

“Good morning,” Jim said cautiously.

“I'm Mara.”

“Jim.”

“I know.”

Jim looked at Kate again.

Kate sighed.

“I may have mentioned you.”

Mara's eyebrows rose.

“May have.”

“Mara.”

“The Louboutins, Kate.”

“They're shoes.”

Jim made a small sound.

Kate turned on him.

“Don't.”

“I didn't say anything.”

“You were about to.”

“I was going to agree with you.”

“No, you weren't.”

“No,” Jim admitted. “I really wasn't.”

Mara laughed.

“Go. Both of you. Before I start charging admission.”

---

They lasted eleven minutes before their first fight.

It was a lamp.

Of course it was a lamp.

Jim stood in front of it with his arms folded.

Kate stood beside him with the expression of a woman witnessing a preventable tragedy.

The lamp had a heavy ceramic base in a muted green glaze and a shade that had clearly been replaced sometime during an administration nobody remembered fondly.

“It has potential,” Jim said.

“It has a hostage situation.”

“The shade is wrong.”

“The lamp is wrong.”

“The proportions are excellent.”

“The proportions are apologizing.”

Jim looked offended.

Kate walked away.

He followed.

“You have no imagination.”

“I have imagination. That's why I can imagine it leaving.”

Three aisles later Kate stopped at a low walnut table.

Jim nearly walked into her.

She touched the edge.

“Oh.”

Jim looked.

“No.”

Kate turned slowly.

“You didn't even look.”

“I looked.”

“You glanced.”

“I processed quickly.”

“This is beautiful.”

“This is a coffee table designed by someone who hates shins.”

“It has clean lines.”

“It has corners sharpened by vengeance.”

Kate crouched slightly to inspect the wood.

Jim watched the dress.

Then the heels.

Then, without saying anything, pulled a folded textile from a nearby stack and laid it over the dusty edge beside her knee before she leaned closer.

Kate stopped.

Looked at the cloth.

Looked at him.

Jim was already studying the table.

“What?”

“Nothing.”

“Why does everyone keep saying that to me today?”

Kate stood.

“Because you're very observant about everything except yourself.”

Jim frowned.

“That sounds made up.”

“It isn't.”

They moved on.

Near the back, Tess Navarro was sorting a box of old paper beneath a handwritten sign asking customers not to reorganize the records unless they were prepared to defend their system publicly.

Kate waved.

Tess lifted two fingers, then immediately looked back down at something in her hand.

“Found trouble?” Kate asked.

“1978 softball banquet program with six phone numbers written on the back.”

Jim leaned in.

“Team roster?”

“Dating strategy.”

Kate nodded solemnly.

“Historical document.”

“Exactly.”

Tess slipped it into a separate folder.

Jim looked at Kate.

“I like her.”

“Everyone likes Tess.”

“I don't.”

The voice came from somewhere behind a massive cabinet.

Jo Mercer emerged carrying a drawer.

Tess didn't look up.

“You brought me coffee this morning.”

“That was workplace safety.”

“You put a heart on the cup.”

“Barista error.”

Jo disappeared again.

Jim watched her go.

“This place is incredible.”

Kate smiled.

“I know.”

And that was the first moment he understood why she'd brought him.

Not because it was funny.

Not because it was lesbian.

Not even because they both liked old things.

Kate knew this place.

Not just the merchandise.

The people.

The rhythms.

The way she moved through it without looking for permission.

Here was another piece of her life that had existed before him.

Jim wanted all of them.

That realization should probably have frightened him.

Instead he saw a ceramic lobster.

“Oh my God.”

Kate turned.

“No.”

“You haven't even seen it.”

“I heard your voice.”

“It has a hat.”

“No.”

“It has a tiny sailor hat.”

“Jim.”

“We can't leave him here.”

“We absolutely can.”

“He's been abandoned.”

“Appropriately.”

Jim picked it up.

Kate stared.

The lobster stared back with the serene confidence of an object that had survived several decades without once deserving to.

Jim held it against his chest.

“His name is Admiral Clawford.”

Kate covered her face.

From the front counter Mara called, “No returns on emotional-support crustaceans.”

Jim called back, “Understood.”

Kate lowered her hands.

“I hate you.”

“No, you don't.”

That landed differently than he'd intended.

They both felt it.

Jim's smile faded into something softer.

Kate looked at him.

“No,” she said.

Quietly.

“I really don't.”

For one second the entire ridiculous store disappeared.

Then Ruth Bell walked past, took one look at the ceramic lobster in Jim's arms, and said, “Eight dollars and I don't want to know.”

Jim brightened.

“Sold.”

Kate stared after Ruth.

“You enable people.”

Ruth kept walking.

“That's how retail works.”

---

They left with the lobster.

Also two linen napkins Kate insisted were too beautiful to abandon, a small framed print they had both reached for at the same time and then argued over who had seen first, and absolutely no furniture.

The print bothered Kate.

Not aesthetically.

Emotionally.

They had both stopped.

Both reached.

Both said, “Oh.”

Same second.

Same instinct.

Jim had noticed too.

He hadn't made anything of it.

That somehow made it worse.

At the car, he put the purchases in the back.

Kate started toward the passenger side.

“Wait.”

Jim opened the trunk again and moved a basket.

Kate looked at the basket.

Then at him.

“What is that?”

“Lunch.”

Kate stared.

“Lunch.”

“Yes.”

“You brought lunch.”

“Yes.”

“How much lunch?”

Jim looked genuinely confused by the question.

“Enough.”

Kate closed her eyes.

“Of course you did.”

He smiled.

“Now where are you taking me?”

Kate opened her eyes.

The teasing eased out of her.

“Somewhere quiet.”

Jim nodded.

No joke.

No push.

“Okay.”

---

Halloway Park changed as they walked.

The more public edges fell behind them first.

Then the open sounds.

Then the sense of being watched by the ordinary movement of other people's days.

The Hollow drew them downward beneath true tree cover, into wooded ravine terrain where the world seemed to close gently rather than claustrophobically.

Jim understood almost immediately.

Kate had gone quieter.

Not sad.

Not tense.

Just less defended.

He carried the basket.

She walked beside him.

At one rough patch in the path, Jim slowed.

Kate looked down at her shoes.

Then at him.

“Don't.”

“I didn't say anything.”

“You're thinking it.”

“I am thinking that those shoes are magnificent.”

Kate blinked.

Jim pointed ahead.

“And that the ground is unworthy of them.”

She laughed.

“There he is.”

He offered his hand.

Not dramatically.

Just there.

Kate took it.

He guided her across the uneven section and let go when the path smoothed.

Neither commented on the fact that her hand had fit into his as though they'd practiced.

Farther in, Kate stopped.

“This is it.”

There was no monument.

No dramatic overlook.

No architectural gesture demanding reverence.

Just forest.

Ravine.

Green filtered light.

Privacy.

A place where St. Claire seemed very far away despite still being around them.

Jim looked at Kate.

“You come here.”

“Yeah.”

“When?”

“When I need everybody to shut up.”

Jim smiled.

“Effective.”

Kate looked through the trees.

“There was a day here once.”

Jim waited.

“It wasn't important.”

He waited some more.

Kate glanced at him.

“You're supposed to ask.”

“You'll tell me if you want to.”

That almost undid her before anything had even happened.

She looked away.

“It was years ago. Nothing happened. That's the whole point.”

Jim set the basket down.

Kate continued.

“I had a day off. The weather was perfect. I got coffee. Walked around St. Claire. Bought something stupid I didn't need. Came here.”

She shrugged.

“I sat for hours.”

Jim listened.

“No crisis. No revelation. Nobody needed anything from me. Nobody was evaluating me. I wasn't proving anything.”

Her voice softened.

“I was just happy.”

Jim looked around.

Kate folded her arms.

“It sounds ridiculous.”

“No.”

“No?”

“No.”

She looked at him.

Jim's expression was completely serious.

“That sounds like a perfect day.”

Kate swallowed.

“Yeah.”

He nodded.

“So this is where you come back to find it.”

Kate's eyes burned.

She hated him.

This was becoming a recurring problem.

“Sometimes.”

Jim bent to the basket.

“Then I brought too much food.”

Kate laughed through the pressure in her chest.

“Obviously.”

---

The tablecloth was handmade.

Kate knew before she asked.

Not because it was imperfect.

Because it wasn't.

It was simply too personal to have come from a store.

Jim spread it over a patch of ground where the surface was level enough, then stopped.

Looked at Kate.

Looked at the dress.

Looked at the cloth.

Without commentary, he repositioned the entire picnic slightly so Kate could sit where the ground beneath the fabric was cleanest and least likely to catch the dress.

Then he took off his lightweight outer layer, folded it, and placed it where her heels could rest without sinking into the softer earth.

Kate stared at him.

Jim looked up.

“What?”

She couldn't speak for a second.

Nothing about the gesture said *you shouldn't have worn those*.

Nothing said *this would be easier if you dressed differently*.

Nothing said *look how gallant I am*.

He had simply looked at the world, looked at Kate as she had chosen to be in it, and quietly adjusted the world where he could.

Kate sat down.

Carefully.

“Thank you.”

Jim shrugged.

“Ground's rude.”

That nearly finished her.

She looked away.

“Food.”

“Yes.”

“Immediately.”

Jim opened the basket.

Abundance emerged.

Frittata.

Pressed sandwiches and stuffed rolls wrapped for the trip.

Marinated vegetables.

Fruit arranged with the kind of care that made grocery-store produce look personally inadequate.

A lemon-and-ricotta sweet that Kate decided not to ask too many questions about because she was already emotionally compromised.

And cloth napkins.

Handmade.

Kate picked one up.

“You made these.”

Jim was unpacking plates.

“Yeah.”

“Why?”

He looked at her.

“Because paper napkins are depressing.”

Kate stared.

Jim paused.

“What?”

“You sew.”

“A straight line isn't sewing.”

“It is literally sewing.”

“It's hemming.”

“With a sewing machine?”

Jim hesitated.

Kate's eyes widened.

“You own a sewing machine.”

“Lots of people own sewing machines.”

“Name three straight men.”

Jim opened his mouth.

Closed it.

Kate smiled.

He pointed a fork at her.

“Technicality.”

She laughed so hard she had to put down the napkin.

There he was.

Carabiner.

Waistcoat.

Gold chain.

Handmade linens.

Masculinity, apparently, had never been safer.

---

The food was extraordinary.

Not restaurant extraordinary.

That wasn't what Jim had made.

It tasted like somebody had learned over years what traveled well, what improved while resting, what could be passed around, what people reached for twice.

Kate ate a piece of frittata and closed her eyes.

“Oh.”

Jim smiled.

“Maria.”

“Nonna Maria?”

“Yeah.”

Kate tried one of the marinated vegetables.

“Jesus.”

“Also Maria.”

“The woman was dangerous.”

“Terrifying.”

He told her just enough.

Maria Russo.

Sicilian.

Heart of the old neighborhood.

Food as abundance rather than display.

Nobody leaving hungry.

Nobody being allowed to pretend they weren't hungry.

Jim had changed things over time.

Kept some.

Lost some.

Made others his.

Kate listened while eating the kind of meal that made conversation slow naturally.

At one point she noticed Jim watching her.

“What?”

“Nothing.”

She narrowed her eyes.

“You hate when I do that.”

“I was just thinking.”

“Dangerous.”

“Extremely.”

He reached into the basket.

And stopped.

Kate saw his expression change.

“Oh no.”

“What?”

“You did flowers.”

Jim looked offended.

“You did flowers.”

Kate had.

Of course she had.

Her arrangement was waiting in the car, because she had decided presenting flowers in the antique store might result in Mara ascending directly into heaven.

Jim pulled his bouquet free.

It wasn't a dictionary exercise.

Not anymore.

The flowers were chosen with the care of someone who knew she would read the sentence because it came from him.

Kate went still.

She looked at the arrangement.

Then at Jim.

He looked suddenly less certain.

“I know we're cheating now.”

“We're not cheating.”

“We've exceeded the available vocabulary.”

“We're developing a dialect.”

Jim smiled.

Kate read it again.

Not species by species.

Him.

The week.

The flowers before these.

Thursday.

Tomorrow.

The Hollow.

The impossible tenderness of a folded jacket beneath her Louboutins.

Her eyes lifted.

“I think this is a dream,” she said softly, “and you're not sure you want to wake up.”

Jim's breath caught.

“Yeah.”

Kate looked down.

“Fuck.”

“Good fuck or bad fuck?”

“Shut up.”

“Understood.”

Kate stood.

Jim blinked.

“Where are you going?”

“To the car.”

“Why?”

“Because apparently we're both idiots.”

She returned several minutes later carrying her own flowers.

Jim stared.

Kate sat again and handed them to him.

His face changed before he even finished looking.

“Oh.”

“Yeah.”

He studied them.

Kate watched him understand.

Not translate.

Understand.

His eyes met hers.

“What the hell do I do to you?”

Kate's throat tightened.

“That.”

Jim looked down at the flowers.

Then back at her.

Neither of them laughed.

For once.

The Hollow held the quiet around them.

Jim reached across the space between them.

Not for her hand.

Not quite.

His fingers touched the edge of the napkin near hers.

Kate moved her hand until her little finger rested against his.

Small.

Almost nothing.

Enough.

Jim looked at the contact.

Then at her.

Kate smiled.

“Still not a date.”

His little finger curled gently around hers.

“Obviously.”

They sat beneath the trees with too much food, two impossible bouquets, a ceramic lobster waiting in the car, and the growing suspicion that neither of them was fooling anyone.

Least of all each other.
